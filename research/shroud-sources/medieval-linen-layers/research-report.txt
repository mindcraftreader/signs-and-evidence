MEDIEVAL ARTISTIC LINEN: PAINT LAYERS AND PENETRATION DEPTH
27 October 2026
Scope:1200–1400 Europe; later comparisons explicitly labeled; not exhaustive.

The search did not recover a representative measured-thickness dataset for free artistic linen from 1200–1400. No defensible same-era mean, standard deviation, rarity probability or universal minimum depth can be computed from the material located.

“Thin” and “surface” describe different things. A paint film above a cloth, paint or binder within weave interstices, colored fibers through a thread, and chemical change within an individual fiber wall need separate measurements. Pigment particle size and overall framed-object thickness are additional, different quantities.

The full 1993 Withyham study now provides a directly verified period example of a thin calcium-sulphate ground and a simple egg-tempera layer structure. It does not provide a numerical depth. The 2017 review describes thick calcium-carbonate ground while citing Villers 2000; since that later chapter remains unavailable, the discrepancy is open and should not be collapsed into one technical finding.

The Monreale panel provides explicit contemporary micron figures, but its linen serves as an interlayer in a rigid, heavily grounded panel. Its20 µm paint maximum does not establish the depth of a thin wash on free linen, nor the colored wall of an individual flax fiber.

The Justus example demonstrates that thin groundless washes can still penetrate the cloth and leave a verso image. It does not show that all medieval washes must penetrate, or that such a wash matches the Shroud. Its support was thought to be linen but not analyzed in that study.

The often-repeated200 nm figure for the Shroud should retain the local0.2 ± 0.2 µm estimate in its primary Fig12 caption. Do not interpret its± notation as an established population standard deviation. The same paper separately discusses thread/fabric superficiality. Its underlying structural interpretation also requires assessment.

The available technique descriptions show a range from mineral grounds filling the weave to glue-sized/no-ground washes. “All medieval linen paintings had thick paint” is not supported. The opposite claim that a documented medieval wash reproduced the Shroud’s exact fiber-scale coloration is also not supported.

No claim of exhaustive coverage is made. Dye-depth studies on garments, non-European cotton prints and ancient Egyptian painted shrouds would expand the question substantially; they cannot silently supply an answer for French 1200–1400 artistic linen.

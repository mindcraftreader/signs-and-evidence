WHO COULD HAVE MADE THE SHROUD?
Research synthesis, 27 October 2026

WORKING CONCLUSION
Status: Inference, conditional on a medieval artificial image

The best-supported maker category in this investigation is an anonymous workshop familiar with Passion imagery, potentially combining sculpture and painting. A French or nearby workshop is a reasonable starting point because of the Lirey acquisition/display setting; French custody does not establish French manufacture. No reviewed source identifies an individual maker. A gifted but otherwise obscure craftsman would fit as well as a famous court artist.

The narrative below is a testable reconstruction: a medieval representation of Christ’s burial cloth was made and acquired for the Lirey setting, then displayed and contested as a relic. Its historical setting is better supported than its exact manufacturing process. It explains why such an object could exist and how some image properties could arise; it does not yet explain every microscopic feature.
Sources: SARZEAUD-2024-TRACE, NICOLOTTI-2025, REPLICA-2010, STURP-1982

1. THE LINEN AND THE COMMISSION
Status: Measured sample age + historical context; commission speculative

The 1988 measurements, published in 1989, give AD 1260–1390 for the sampled linen at the stated 95% confidence. If that strip represents the main cloth, and the conventional first Lirey displays around 1355 are correctly reconstructed, an artificial image could have been made in the late thirteenth or first half of the fourteenth century. Choosing about 1330–1355 is an illustrative scenario, not a new measured image date. Carbon dates flax, not the moment of image formation.

A church or aristocratic patron could commission a large Passion object meant to evoke the burial of Christ. Our large-linen collection shows that large medieval devotional textiles existed, but does not establish a stock supplier of identical herringbone linen. Princeton’s 1300–1350 epitaphios supplies a narrower contextual point: a cloth showing the dead Christ could have a devotional function without purporting to be his actual burial cloth. It is Byzantine embroidery, not a French technical prototype.
Sources: C14-1989, EPITAPHIOS-PRINCETON, SARZEAUD-2024-TRACE

2. A WORKSHOP DESIGNS THE BODY IMAGE
Status: Plausible partial mechanism, not a recovered medieval procedure

A sculptor could supply shallow models or a controlled surface for the face and body, while a painter planned the paired front/back arrangement and Passion details. Relief contact or rubbing can make projecting features darker, creating a negative-like tonal pattern without photographic equipment. That observation makes an extraordinary photographic invention unnecessary for this particular property; it does not establish which process made Turin’s image.

Garlaschelli’s 2010 physical experiments combined body rubbing with a relief for the face and manual additions. They demonstrate a practical route to several broad visual effects. Moraes’s 2025 digital study separately shows why a flattened relief can avoid some distortions of a draped full-volume model. Its model is adjusted using the target image, and its cloth uses default cotton settings. It tests selected geometry rather than medieval craftsmanship, image chemistry or every possible body-to-cloth mechanism.
Sources: REPLICA-2010, MORAES-2025

3. MAKING A FAINT IMAGE THAT SURVIVES
Status: The principal unresolved technical step

A contact-assisted surface reaction, possibly followed by removal of loose material, is one candidate. It could in principle leave a faint image with little surviving binder. This is a hypothesis, not evidence of a historical washing or aging treatment. Garlaschelli’s effective chemical trials used an aqueous acid/pigment slurry, oven treatment and washing; a proposed centuries-long natural-aging equivalent has not been demonstrated simply by doing that accelerated experiment.

The obstacle is the physical match. Schwalbe and Rogers interpreted the main image as altered cellulose and found serious difficulties for conventional paint and other tested mechanisms. Fanti and Heimburger argue that the Garlaschelli replica misses important fiber-level and image/blood properties. McCrone instead argued for pigments and tempera. Those incompatible interpretations cannot be silently combined into a proven recipe. A convincing photograph of a replica is insufficient; a proposed process must pass the microscopic and chemical comparisons too.
Sources: REPLICA-2010, STURP-1982, FANTI-HEIMBURGER-2011, VIKAN-1998

4. WOUNDS AND BLOOD ARE A SEPARATE OPERATION
Status: Observed-material interpretations; sequence remains a constraint

An artificial-image scenario can include real blood, a blood substitute, or different materials in different marks; none should be asserted without tests. Heller and Adler interpreted sampled red material as blood-related. Their protease observations on selected fibers support a blood-before-image interpretation in those samples, rather than a rule that every red mark was added last. Garlaschelli’s later-added surrogate blood does not settle that sequence.

Real human blood, if confirmed, would not by itself identify the person or date the image. Conversely, calling all the red marks paint would dismiss contrary analyses without resolving them. There is no evidence here for a murdered model or a staged medieval crucifixion, and this narrative does not require one.
Sources: CHEMISTRY-1981, REPLICA-2010, FANTI-HEIMBURGER-2011

5. ACQUISITION AND THE CHANGE FROM IMAGE TO RELIC
Status: Retrospective documentary allegations; intention unknown

The conventional c.1355 beginning is reconstructed from later documents. In the 1389–1390 controversy, Bishop Pierre d’Arcis alleged earlier manufacture and investigation under his predecessor Henri of Poitiers. The record points to the dean, Robert de Caillac, as procuring the object. It reports an artisan’s admission but does not preserve a named maker’s signed confession, examination protocol or recipe. The bishop’s report is valuable evidence requiring source criticism, not a modern laboratory result.

This leaves two plausible branches: someone commissioned a counterfeit relic, or a representation originally made for devotion was subsequently promoted as an authentic relic. Nicolotti explicitly distinguishes procurement from the unknown artist’s intention. Charny’s association with the foundation and cloth does not prove that he ordered a forgery. Poulle’s criticism of editions and papal drafts is also a reason to avoid using every accusatory phrase as an uncontested fact.

The papal handling permitted a representation to be displayed under restrictions; it was not a scientific authentication. Subsequent custody and prestige could sustain veneration without preserving the maker’s name. That social process explains the loss of an artistic attribution more readily than it supplies a chemical explanation for the image.
Sources: NICOLOTTI-2025, POULLE-2006, SARZEAUD-2024-TRACE

A PUBLISHED ROYAL-COURT NARRATIVE TO INVESTIGATE
Status: Published hypothesis, not independently verified here

Gary Vikan’s 2020 publisher synopsis proposes a court artist during the Black Death, a gift from John II to Geoffroi de Charny, and a devotional object later treated as a relic. This is a useful version of the workshop narrative to test against archives. Only the synopsis, not the full book, was reviewed here. I have not independently recovered a contemporary royal gift or payment record establishing that chain, so those attractive details remain outside the factual backbone.
Sources: VIKAN-2020

WHAT THIS LEAVES OPEN
Status: Alternatives and limits

A medieval object could have been imported before reaching Lirey; the place of display does not establish place of manufacture. An older cloth could also carry a later image. An authentic ancient cloth under the repaired-corner hypothesis is a different explanation: it requires independent support that the dated strip fails to represent the main linen. The workshop reconstruction does not itself disprove that repair claim, and the repair claim does not identify an image mechanism.

The newer aging-proxy dates must retain their calibration, provenance and shared-environment uncertainties from our dating audit; they are not extra independent votes whose standard deviations can simply be multiplied. None of the historical, replication or geometric studies reviewed here resolves that dependence. The present conclusion is therefore a plausible partial creation narrative, with an anonymous maker and a substantial open chemical step, rather than a solved attribution.
Sources: ROGERS-2005, C14-1989, MULTIMETHOD-2015, MORAES-2025

WHO TO INVESTIGATE

Anonymous workshop | Best current category | Could combine design, relief carving, surface treatment and finishing. No commission, model or recipe connects a workshop securely to the cloth.

Évrard d’Orléans and circle | First named comparison lead | Painter/sculptor active at the right time, with religious commissions including a cross and commemorative image. Capability and chronology fit; no reviewed Shroud, Charny or Lirey connection.

Jean Pépin de Huy and collaborators | Relevant funerary workshop comparison | Pre-Lirey recumbent and funerary sculpture. No link to the cloth or its surface chemistry.

Ravy / Bouteiller / Notre-Dame workshop | Relevant relief comparison | Christ relief production before 1351. A skill comparison, without acquisition or manufacturing evidence.

Robert de Caillac | Documentary procurement lead | The dean is implicated in the later bishop’s account. A purchaser/exhibitor lead, not an identified sculptor or chemist.

Geoffroi de Charny / family | Patronage and custody lead | Important setting for acquisition and display; does not establish who commissioned or made the image.

Beauneveu, Jean de Liège, Sluter / Marville | Later comparisons | The reviewed major works postdate the conventional first Lirey display; they cannot be credited from those works alone.

EVIDENCE MATRIX

Why make a body image on cloth? | Period devotional and Passion-image contexts support a motive. | Supported context | Does not establish an original liturgical use for Turin.

Why the paired front/back form? | A planned representation could imitate a folded burial cloth. | Inference | No preparatory drawing or exact model recovered.

Negative-like tones and some relief effects | Contact/rubbing and selected relief geometry can generate related effects. | Partial demonstrations | Not an exact whole-image or fiber-level replication.

Faint, superficial yellow body image | A localized surface reaction is a candidate. | Unresolved mechanism | No verified medieval recipe matching all physical evidence.

Red marks and their sequence | A separate wound operation is possible. | Contested material/sequence constraints | Replica surrogate marks do not establish original blood chemistry or chronology.

Medieval linen date | The representative-sample scenario fits the workshop account. | Measurement with sampling condition | Does not separately date the image; repaired-corner claim needs direct evidence.

Arrival at Lirey | Retrospective documents provide a procurement/display setting. | Historical evidence requiring criticism | No named maker, surviving original confession or certain intended deception.

A particular famous artist | Some artists demonstrably had relevant skills. | Comparison only | No discriminating template match, payment or provenance chain.

NEXT TESTS

1. Archive first: trace the dean’s procurement network, Charny connections and relevant church/court payments. A direct commission or inventory description would discriminate better than general facial resemblance.
2. Compare distinctive design features against securely dated reliefs and drawings, with alternative workshops and controls. Beard, crossed hands and a recumbent Christ are too general for attribution.
3. Run physical tests with period-appropriate linen, reliefs and full-volume controls; measure fabric properties and vary tension/pressure rather than adopting generic cotton defaults.
4. Test plausible reagents with untreated controls and several aging conditions. Do not assume an oven exposure equals centuries of natural aging.
5. Define image comparisons in advance: fiber-color depth and distribution, fluorescence, pigment/binder residues, edge behavior, noncontact image and blood/image sequence. Use independent blinded assessments.
6. If future custodial access permits, compare multiple cloth locations and the dated corner by thread structure, chemistry and dating. This investigation made no sampling or access request.

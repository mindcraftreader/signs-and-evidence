ZEITOUN ORDINARY CAUSE AUDIT
28 October 2026

Conclusion for the parent article

The strongest defensible ordinary explanation is genuinely visible lights, of unresolved physical origin, interpreted through an already circulating Marian expectation. There is direct evidence for the interpretation component in some episodes. There is no demonstrated physical explanation of the complete early record of prolonged human forms and purposeful gestures. It would be false to claim that Zeitoun has been explained as headlights, a projector, mass hallucination, or earthquake lights. It would also be false to claim that perception and social influence have no evidential foothold here.

The defensible case against treating supernatural causation as established is therefore an underdetermination argument supported by actual perceptual evidence, not a completed alternative causal reconstruction. Whether that is enough to prefer an ordinary explanation depends partly on the credibility assigned to the stronger shape-and-gesture testimony and on prior expectations. This audit does not produce numerical odds or establish ordinary causes as more likely than all supernatural explanations.

Ready to adapt into article prose

At Zeitoun, the best ordinary case does not require thousands of people to invent a luminous scene independently. A common external light could be visible to a crowd while its form, movement and identity were partly constructed in perception. That distinction matters because a photographed light and sincere agreement that it was Mary would then be expected together. They would not, by themselves, establish that the light was a supernatural person.

This possibility has a concrete foothold. American University in Cairo anthropologist Cynthia Nelson attended in 1968 and later published field notes. She observed a white-shirted person initially greeted as the apparition before the crowd corrected itself. On another visit she could trace a nunlike outline in a light among tree branches when looking for that figure; the outline disappeared when she regarded the scene as an illusion. On June 1 she recorded spectators identifying intermittent flashes as Mary. Crucially, she also acknowledged disconnected surrounding streetlights and did not identify the source of the mysterious light. She asked about headlights; she did not prove headlights caused it. [Nelson, printed pp. 5–6, 8.]

These observations do not merely invoke a general human capacity for error. They show that some actual Zeitoun identifications were responsive to interpretation and crowd signals. They justify separating the physical stimulus from what spectators understood it to be. They do not establish that the sustained figures described by other witnesses were the same perceptual experience. The early committee account and contemporary statements attributed to Bishop Athanasius describe more: a complete moving form, bowing at the cross, and gestures toward the crowd. If those descriptions accurately capture a sustained, externally resolved figure, the simple ambiguous-light model remains incomplete.

Cross-religious witnesses strengthen the case that the phenomenon was public and was not confined to a small private visionary group. They do not supply an experiment free of shared expectations. Egyptian Muslims also honored Mary, and Nelson records a Muslim observer who understood the apparition within her own religion. That makes common religious interpretation possible without a conspiracy. It does not explain away a luminous figure above a Christian church, and it does not remove the evidential significance of reported bows toward its cross.

The government response adds real historical weight, especially the contemporary civil report describing lighting checks. But a narrative of disabled local lights cannot do the work of a disclosed technical exclusion of every artificial source. The exact district-wide blackout, measured radius, hours-long duration, exhaustive balcony searches, Nasser's personal confirmation, and discounted land sale have now been withheld under the user's agreed rule. Their absence from the admitted case leaves physical alternatives incompletely tested; it is not evidence that a hidden apparatus existed. Crowd-management action is evidence of a substantial public gathering, not an independent measurement of the cause of the light.

An earthquake-light proposal is more specific than simply saying something natural might have happened. John S. Derr and Laurentian University psychologist Michael A. Persinger reported a monthly correlation between sightings and regional seismicity in 1989. But their abstract places the major seismic increase roughly 400 km away and about a year after the appearances began. It identifies an association, not an observed light-generating process at the church; it does not demonstrate a human figure or its reported gestures. It should be mentioned as a hypothesis, not used as the article's solution.

The strongest critical conclusion is consequently narrower than a debunking: the record contains good evidence of a public luminous phenomenon and serious early testimony to a religiously meaningful figure, but it also contains directly observed interpretive ambiguity and lacks the measurements that would settle which descriptions correspond to which external scene. These facts support withholding a verdict; they do not establish that Mary did not appear. Conversely, the failure to complete an ordinary reconstruction does not by itself identify the agency or establish Catholic doctrinal vindication. The proponent's argument is stronger when it rests on the positive religious and testimonial pattern rather than on an alleged elimination of every physical source.

Photo inference
Existing local material records Al-Ahram's examination of Wagih Rizk's original negative and finding of no photomontage, plus later physicist John Jackson's consistency assessment. Authenticity of a negative would strengthen the inference that a real luminous scene was photographed; it is not by itself a determination of the source of that luminosity or its agency. Avoid treating all pictures as fraudulent, or conversely treating a photography-department finding as proof of supernatural cause. No new negative, calibrated exposure record, stereoscopic geometry, or time-synchronized independent photographs were obtained in this audit.

Warnings for the parent article
- Do not say Nelson proved headlights; she did not.
- Do not use the unvalidated five stronger government details, on either side, as established facts.
- Do not say expecting Mary implies imagining everything, or that all reports are copies.
- Do not say Muslim witnesses cannot be subject to Marian expectations; do not call them uniformly hostile witnesses either.
- Do not infer no figure existed because one witness failed to see one on selected visits.
- Do not claim tectonic strain is a demonstrated source of the Zeitoun light.
- Do not claim perfect experiential unanimity is necessary for a real public event.
- Do not substitute very large crowd estimates for a count of independently preserved descriptions of the same episode.
- Do not introduce medical evidence or its weaknesses into this nonmedical argument.
- Do not let the article's title promise a disproval that its reasoning does not deliver.

Suggested concluding judgment
I can defend a serious evidence-based case for withholding supernatural attribution at Zeitoun. I cannot defend the claim that the event has been adequately explained by an identified ordinary cause. The first conclusion does not entail the second, and the failure of the second does not by itself prove the supernatural alternative. The cumulative case must compare the positive likelihoods of the stronger observed religious pattern under both accounts, without making either an unfalsifiable default.

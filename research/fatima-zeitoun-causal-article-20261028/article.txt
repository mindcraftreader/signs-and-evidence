The case for withholding supernatural attribution at Fatima and Zeitoun
An assessment for Daniel
28 October 2026

Fátima and Zeitoun supply substantial positive evidence for a Marian interpretation. I nevertheless withhold assent to the conclusion that supernatural Marian intervention is their best-supported cause. My objection concerns the distance between what the surviving records securely establish and the more specific physical claims on which the strongest causal argument depends. Ordinary mechanisms have some direct evidential support. A complete ordinary reconstruction of both cases has not been established. Both statements matter to my judgment.

This article addresses Daniel's strongest argument: an advance promise of a public evidentiary sign at Fátima, striking experiences reported by contemporary journalists and distant witnesses, and repeated publicly reported Marian appearances at Zeitoun. It does not require every observer to see the same thing, assume that the astronomical Sun physically moved, or use healing claims that were never central to his case. It also carries evidence between the cases rather than resetting the discussion to a world in which miracles are presumed impossible.

The standard is preponderance of the evidence: which causal interpretation deserves greater confidence, without requiring certainty? An ordinary explanation can reasonably include a physical stimulus, perception, communication and memory. It must earn that complexity by fitting the actual records. A Marian explanation can unify religiously specific features through purposeful agency. It must likewise be judged against the record, including inconvenient observations. Neither explanation wins by being named.

Fatima and the prediction

The advance promise survives scrutiny. The critical edition published by the Sanctuary of Fátima, with scholarly oversight associated with the Portuguese Catholic University's Centre for Religious History Studies, preserves a 14 July 1917 interrogation recording a promised miracle that would make people believe. Manuel Nunes Formigão's 27 September interrogation connects the coming 13 October appearance with belief in the Lady's appearances. A skeptic cannot fairly say that the entire prediction was invented after the event. Its evidentiary purpose was already present. [1]

That is a serious point for Daniel. A public event on an announced date is more significant than an unexplained experience later attached to a religion. The skeptical reply must nevertheless consider what the advance promise specified. The inspected passages do not predict a sequence of rotations and descents, particular colors, a measured trajectory or the illumination geometry of a photograph. A dated promise of a sign leaves more interpretive room than a precise description of a physical event recorded before it occurred. [1]

The announcement also helped produce the observing conditions: people assembled expecting an extraordinary event and attended to the sky. That is an actual causal link, not an allegation that all witnesses fabricated their experiences. It means that the prediction and the crowd's later interpretation cannot be treated as unrelated draws whose improbabilities are simply multiplied. This weakens a particular coincidence argument while preserving the genuine force of the announced date.

Avelino de Almeida's contemporary reporting in O Século deserves weight. His willingness to describe an extraordinary experience despite the newspaper's political and religious setting makes casual dismissal implausible. But credibility about an experience does not turn a visual description into a measurement of its external cause. A reporter can truthfully describe apparent motion while the causal question remains whether the stimulus moved, viewing conditions changed, or perception contributed to the apparent movement. [2]

Geographical corroboration raises the evidentiary stakes. The retrieved record includes a next-day letter relaying observations at Granja and a November 1917 letter relaying a less dramatic phenomenon at Torres Novas. Those are early reports. They are also indirect reports of the distant observers, rather than measurements from those observers themselves. The most explicitly Fátima-directed descent account we retrieved, Albano Barros's, comes through John Haffert's much later interview publication. Some other detailed distant accounts likewise survive through later correspondence or recollections. [3, 4]

This matters because several independent lines of sight converging on one nearby moving object would be much harder to explain through individual solar perception. Our map establishes the distribution of reported localities. It does not yet establish measured convergence: the records generally lack exact observing positions, azimuths and synchronized times. Calling that distinction merely a demand for certainty would miss its purpose. It changes what physical fact the distant testimony currently establishes. Even without triangulation or the photographic argument, the conjunction of the advance promise and the early extraordinary-experience reports remains substantial evidence requiring an answer.

Nor were all distant spectators necessarily unsuspecting controls. In the Alburitel hill-group account preserved by Diogo Pacheco de Amorim, the University of Coimbra mathematician, people anticipated a celestial sign and prepared smoked glass. That is direct evidence of expectation in one distant group. It cannot be extended to every remote witness. It does, however, defeat the blanket assumption that geographical distance automatically made every observation independent of the announcement. [3]

What ordinary vision can and cannot explain

There is experimental evidence that ordinary perception can supply colors that are not present in the stimulus. Qasim Zaidi and Robert Ennis of SUNY College of Optometry, with Dingcai Cao of the University of Chicago and Barry Lee of SUNY and the Max Planck Institute, measured color afterimages at moderate light levels. Their 2012 Current Biology paper connects perceptual results with retinal neural responses. It shows that adaptation-based color effects do not universally require extreme light exposure or extensive photopigment bleaching. [5]

That result bears on one part of the dispute. Comfortable viewing does not, by itself, rule out every adaptation-based color effect. The experiment does not establish the illumination conditions at Fátima, reproduce its reported descents, explain every claim of environmental color change, or identify what each witness saw. I will not describe a laboratory color effect as a laboratory reconstruction of the miracle.

Auguste Meessen, writing from the Institute of Physics at the Catholic University of Louvain, proposed a more extensive physiological account in conference proceedings. His personal experiments produced disc and color effects, but he explicitly reported that he did not see the movements. His treatment of rotation, oscillation and apparent approach therefore goes beyond what his personal experiment reproduced. This is a proposed ordinary mechanism with partial observational support, not a completed explanation of Fátima. [6]

Philippe Dalleur of the Pontifical University of the Holy Cross offers a potentially more discriminating argument: infer the illumination direction in a historical photograph from object-shadow correspondences, then compare it with the expected Sun position. His method has worked examples, including real photographs. Successful geometry in those examples is relevant evidence. Its accuracy in the contested Fátima photograph still depends on identifying faint shadow boundaries correctly and accounting for the combined uncertainties. [7]

The counterarguments we reviewed expose specific possible failures of feature selection and mixed illumination. They have not supplied a completed ordinary-sun reconstruction fitting all the features Dalleur identifies. I therefore cannot say that his result has been debunked. I also cannot use the claimed discrepancy as a settled measurement of a supernatural local emitter without a sufficiently robust estimate of feature-selection and illumination uncertainties. The photographic argument deserves provisional evidential weight; a dispute alone does not cancel it. [7, 8]

The preceding rain and wet conditions are well attested, and the reports of rapid drying deserve to be assessed as physical claims. Visual adaptation cannot dry clothing. If the accounts establish the extraordinarily rapid drying of soaked clothes and ground, an ordinary model needs a further physical explanation. The photographed tonal patterns may corroborate differential wetness, but do not independently measure the elapsed drying time or initial water content. I have not supplied an adequate physical reconstruction of the strongest drying reports. They remain an affirmative challenge to my countercase. [7, 8]

Zeitoun and the evidence for perceptual interpretation

Zeitoun has affirmative evidence beyond recent devotional retellings. William Farid Basili, Director of Inquiries and Complaints in Egypt's Ministry of Tourism, issued a report dated 30 April 1968 describing the Virgin's appearances and attempts to eliminate nearby lighting as the explanation. Contemporary named clergy and other witnesses describe sustained figures and purposeful gestures. The religious interpretation therefore draws on reported content, not merely the absence of an ordinary explanation. [9, 10]

Cynthia Nelson, an anthropologist at the American University in Cairo, supplies concrete counterevidence. She recorded a white-shirted person initially mistaken for something extraordinary, followed by correction. On another visit she perceived a nunlike outline when trying to picture one; it disappeared when she regarded the light as an illusion. She also recorded spectators identifying intermittent flashes as Mary. These are observed instances of misidentification and interpretation at the site. [11]

Nelson also acknowledged disconnected streetlights and left the light source unexplained. She did not demonstrate headlights. Her observations support a perceptual contribution to some episodes, while leaving other witnesses’ sustained-figure accounts to be evaluated on their own evidence. [11]

The ordinary candidate with real evidential support is consequently visible light of unresolved origin combined, in at least some observations, with interpretation shaped by attention and surrounding people. This explains why a sincerely perceived figure need not settle the form of the external stimulus. It remains incomplete on the origin, persistence and behavior of the light and on the strongest articulated-figure accounts. Those are substantial remaining obligations for an ordinary explanation.

Muslim testimony corroborates experiences beyond a Christian congregation. Nelson’s interviews show that a Marian interpretation was compatible with some Muslim observers’ beliefs. The reported initial recognition of an ordinary woman and later descriptions of bowing toward a cross still deserve separate weight; cross-confessional testimony strengthens the case without establishing expectation-free observation. [11]

We have agreed to withhold five stronger claims pending validation: a measured suburb-wide grid cutoff lasting hours, forty officers every night, exhaustive rooftop and balcony searches, Nasser's personal witnessing and confirmation, and a discounted land sale. Their exclusion does not erase the narrower lighting checks or documented government response. The cutoff and search claims, if validated, would strengthen the exclusion of some artificial-light explanations. Their currently unverified scope cannot be treated as an exhaustive experimental exclusion. [12]

What the combined argument establishes

Combining the cases is legitimate. Portugal in 1917 and Egypt in 1968 present different settings and different reported phenomena. No located document establishes that Zeitoun's original event was copied from Fátima. A shared Marian tradition supplies an interpretive context; it does not supply a complete common ordinary cause. If Zeitoun adds reliably established observations that are difficult under ordinary explanations, it must increase the cumulative weight for Marian intervention.

The cumulative argument nevertheless depends on the level at which the observations are established. A predicted occasion of collective extraordinary visual experience and a predicted independently located physical light source are different evidentiary claims. Repeated reports of a luminous figure and separately recorded, sufficiently detailed observations corroborating the same external movements are likewise different claims. The stronger versions would favor purposeful agency more sharply. The surviving sources support parts of those stronger versions, but the remaining source and measurement issues affect precisely the features doing the most causal work.

That is why the uncertainty is not answered simply by adding more spectators. A crowd can provide genuinely corroborative observations. Its members may also share one stimulus and exchange interpretations, so the number present does not determine the number of independent tests of every reported feature. Equally, identifying some dependence does not justify assigning the whole crowd zero weight.

Nor does the combined ordinary account require every improbable exception to occur independently. A common viewing condition can explain several linked perceptual effects; an announced sign can explain gathering, attention and interpretive agreement together. The supernatural account can also unify these features. Counting a separate low probability for each description would prejudge which features share a cause. We do not have justified numerical probabilities for either model, and I will not invent them.

The specifically Catholic vindication in Daniel's argument rests principally on Fátima's reported message and public sign. Zeitoun could corroborate Christian Marian intervention despite its Coptic setting and silence. That distinction is already part of his argument. It would be evasive to accept a genuine Marian authentication of Fátima and then demand separate proof of every Catholic doctrine before allowing the narrower phrase Catholic vindicatory miracle. My unresolved question precedes that step: whether the events are better attributed to Marian agency in the first place.

My judgment and the concession I owe

At a preponderance standard, my present judgment remains suspended. The reason is that the causal ranking is sensitive to how much confidence the strongest figure, motion, location and lighting reports deserve. Taking their most discriminating physical content as reliably established makes Marian intervention substantially more compelling. Giving greater weight to the documented perceptual ambiguity and transmission limits makes an ordinary account more compelling. Our investigation has not supplied a sufficiently stable basis for me to defend a balance-of-probabilities ranking of all ordinary alternatives together against Marian intervention. That is an assessment of this record, not a demand that historical claims attain certainty.

I concede that Daniel has established a serious cumulative case containing positive evidence for a supernatural Christian explanation. I also concede that I have not demonstrated a complete ordinary explanation for Fátima, Zeitoun or their combination, and cannot honestly present skepticism as the established winner. The article's concrete counterarguments justify continued withholding of assent; they do not constitute a decisive naturalistic refutation.

I do not therefore assert that we have established a reality containing Catholic vindicatory miracles. The choice is not exhausted by a demonstrated ordinary cause and a demonstrated supernatural cause. A historical causal question can remain undecided even when one explanation is intelligible, evidentially supported and reasonably believed. Daniel may reasonably judge that the affirmative case crosses his threshold. My present conclusion does not. The concessions above describe the ground his argument has actually gained.

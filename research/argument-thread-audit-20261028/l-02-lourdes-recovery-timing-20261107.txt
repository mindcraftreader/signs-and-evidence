L-02 | LOURDES | TIMING OF THE STRONGEST RAPID RECOVERIES
7 November 2026

Challenge and result

The assistant proposed that ordinary recoveries plus observation and reporting
around a Lourdes pilgrimage could account for the timing of the strongest
recoveries. Daniel asked the assistant to proceed. This tests the chronology
of the sustained shrine-associated pattern. It leaves the clinical grading
separate and does not substitute a test of an individual diagnosis for the
larger claim.

The Bureau's case selection and some published summaries favor sharp ritual
timing. That is a concrete limitation on some reported onset times. Repeated
observations at the shrine and named eyewitness accounts also survive the
timing check. The clinical comparators do not establish the expected frequency
of these changes during equally observed weeks, or account for the hardest
cases. The assistant withdraws the challenge as presented.

Method

I used all ten preexisting solid-before-and-after fast entries, with no grade
changes. For each I separated the earliest and latest recorded observations of
the graded change from the testimony locating the onset at a rite. Reports
reproduced in later publications retain their actual provenance. I counted
clinical or nursing observations made on both sides while at Lourdes separately
from cases whose graded clinical measurements bracket the trip. A lay witness
can give important positive evidence within a wider clinical window.

Seven of the ten entries link to a visit to the French shrine. Three concern
water or devotion elsewhere. Three of the seven have dated clinical or
nursing observations on both sides of a recorded change at Lourdes. The four
others have broader medical windows, often with additional firsthand evidence
inside the window. These are selected evidence cases. The counts cannot be
interpreted as Lourdes incidence or as a representative sample of all cures.

The seven French-shrine cases

• Marie Borel, 1907. The detailed narrative published in the BMJ reports
  active drainage at the morning dressing on August 21, four dry sites at
  dressing removal before that afternoon's immersion, and all six closed
  by the Bureau exam the next day. Guinier personally observed the dry
  healing sites and reexamined her in 1908. Guinier dated the cure to the
  baths on August 22. The difference shows how a staged change can acquire
  one ritual time. The dated dressings still put major external closure
  during the visit. Internal tract healing and epithelial repair are
  distinct from the timing of cessation of discharge.
  https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/2/2590/467.full.pdf (printed p. 470)
  https://archive.org/details/etudes121jesu (Guinier, printed p. 595)

• John Traynor, 1923. Vallet's 1926 publication reproduces the Liverpool
  physicians' examinations of July 24, 25 and 27 at Lourdes. They recorded
  absent voluntary leg movement on the 24th, leg movement and painful,
  difficult walking after the procession on the 25th, and normal walking
  and recovered arm use on the 27th. These are three distinct observations,
  independent of the date the Bureau took up the case. Residual hand signs
  and the absence of paired anatomical tests limit tissue-regrowth claims.
  The documents support rapid functional improvement within the pilgrimage.
  https://www.lourdes-france.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/ATTACHMENT-1.A-JOURNAL-Pages-entieres-DE-LA-GROTTE-DE-LOURDES_1926_52.pdf

• Marie Seguin, 1891. The Annales still records moist chest sounds on
  August 22 despite the reported return of energy at the bath. The sounds
  had disappeared by August 24, with a slight residual finding. The two
  observations are on site. They distinguish the time she felt better
  from the observed change in auscultation. They do not establish the
  timing of microbiological clearance or confirm the original TB diagnosis.
  Annales August 1891, printed p. 98, in the existing 1891 audit.

• Amélie Chagnon, 1891. Dupont saw her suffering the eve of departure,
  reported persistent drainage and recorded a firm scar on return in his
  August 30 certificate. His later quoted recollection specifies a shorter
  interval of roughly four days from departure to reexamination. Gaillard
  certified free motion and a firm scar on September 5. Boissarie reproduces
  the named bath attendant de la Salinière's account of seeing the wound
  between the immersions and a pink scar immediately after the second,
  and reports Bureau examination about half an hour later. The bath timing
  has direct witness support, with a wider interval in the dated certificates.
  https://archive.org/details/b24861820/page/116/mode/2up (printed pp. 115–119)

• Joachime Dehant, 1878. Froidebise's September 6 and 19 certificates,
  reproduced by Le Bec, bracket an extensive ulcer and a scar. Léonie
  Dorval's reported testimony describes seeing the wound during the
  September 11–12 journey and the changed surface on removing the bandage
  after a bath. The modern official summary and Le Bec differ on whether
  the recovery date was September 13 or 15. The cited companion narrows
  the time beyond the certificates. The exact hour and depth of repair
  remain uncertain.
  https://archive.org/details/medicalproofofmi00leberich (printed pp. 146–151)

• Marie Lemarchand, 1892. La Néele describes prior draining lesions and
  persistence to departure. On August 26 after her return he found dry
  wounds with pink scar tissue and restored motor function. The reported
  bath date is August 21. The original published chapter itself proposes
  a functional explanation for some neurological symptoms, distinguishing
  the physical wounds. It also reproduces d'Hombres's post-bath inspection
  of the changed surface. The treating doctor's paired examinations
  confirm the rapid external change without measuring the minute of onset
  or settling the original anatomical diagnosis.
  https://archive.org/details/twentycuresatlou0000drfd (printed pp. 23–30)

• Jeanne Frétel, 1948. Pellé's Rennes temperature measurements show large
  swings before departure and a steadier trace on return. I visually checked
  the reproduced chart. There is a gap through the trip, including the
  reported October 8 Communion. Roques describes seeing her open her eyes
  and later eat. The file contains a late version of his statement and
  records that investigators took testimony from him shortly after the
  event. The October 9 Bureau exam independently documents walking and a
  soft, painless abdomen. The chart establishes a turn across the journey.
  It gives no temperature at the moment of Communion. The commission
  reports that the spring course of streptomycin ended about four months
  earlier, with severe symptoms and fever persisting before departure.
  https://doi.org/10.3917/socio.023.0243 (documents 2–4, 6 and 8)

The three other locations

• Frère Adolphe, 1891. Carrier's November certificate gives a sudden
  improvement after oral Lourdes water in Lyon on August 15, with food
  tolerated before dawn and continued improvement. The community superior
  gives the immediate sequence. Carrier himself proposed a neurotic
  mechanism for part of the turnaround and considered its severity unusual.
• Sœur Labadie, 1891. Pansini describes convalescence around the thirteenth
  day of the acute illness at Molfetta, Italy. A Lourdes novena was in
  progress. Pre-antibiotic clinical descriptions of a pneumonia crisis
  substantiate rapid defervescence as an ordinary course in some serious
  chest infections. The surviving description does not determine the pathogen.
  https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1914.02560290004002
• Marguerite Verzier, 1909. Tournaire's July 7 and 9 examinations at La
  Teppe document a change in mobility at an old fracture site and ability
  to walk with support. The case is linked to devotion away from the
  shrine. Later radiography cannot date the start of osseous union.
  https://archive.org/details/medicalproofofmi00leberich (printed pp. 131–140)

How far the ordinary comparisons go

• Härle and colleagues found 30 spontaneous enterocutaneous fistula
  closures in 187 patients in a modern population-based cohort. The
  median time from onset to closure was three months, and a closure was
  recorded as late as 17 months. The Visschers clinical series recorded
  23 of 79 with spontaneous closure during active conservative care.
  These results defeat a categorical claim that a longstanding fistula
  cannot close without an operation. They do not give the time from last
  fecal discharge to a sealed outlet, identify Borel's internal anatomy,
  or supply a comparable 1907 risk over the two observed days.
  https://doi.org/10.1111/codi.17186
  https://doi.org/10.1007/s00268-012-1663-4
• First World War military physicians reported rapid return of movement
  in patients diagnosed with functional paraplegia. This gives an ordinary
  example for an endpoint like walking, while furnishing neither a
  patient-specific diagnosis nor a joint explanation of Traynor's course.
  https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/2/2961/409.full.pdf (printed p. 411)
• Clinical recordings show that chest crackles can change appreciably in
  two to four days during recovery from pneumonia. This is a reasonable
  comparator for the changing sign in Seguin, without establishing her
  diagnosis or long-term outcome. Contemporary studies also found rapid
  responses of peritoneal TB to streptomycin, but the Frétel record places
  her sustained turnaround months after the course ended, with continued
  illness in between. The comparison does not match her chronology.
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1623749/
  https://doi.org/10.1016/0002-9343(50)90225-7

Can the frequency be tested with the surviving counts?

The Sanctuary explicitly favors sudden recoveries for recognition. François,
Sternberg and Fee's historical archive study describes 59% of 382 selected
reports with adequate information as reported instantaneous. Table 1 contains
305 of its 411 improvements or cures in the recovery year's Bureau registry,
78 in later years' registers and 28 additional Annales reports. This
documents the distinction between registry year and reported recovery year.
It does not estimate when all recoveries began or how often patients would
recover in a comparable week.
Dowling's 1984 account gives a rough estimate of over two million sick
pilgrimages/visitors by that date, without case-specific person-time,
diagnoses, treatment or observation windows. The later prospective
questionnaire measured self-rated quality of life and precluded clinical
assessment of disease severity. I found no diagnosis-matched cohort with
equivalent observation inside and outside a pilgrimage window in the
sources searched.

The published figures constrain reporting and case selection. They supply
neither an ordinary expected count for rapid changes like Borel's nor
a measured shrine-specific recovery rate. Strong cases remain even after
separating recorded clinical timing from the reported ritual moment.
The selected ten remain in their existing forensic grades.

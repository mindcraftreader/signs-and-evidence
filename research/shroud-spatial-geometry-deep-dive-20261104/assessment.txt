SHROUD SPATIAL GEOMETRY: WHAT THE RECONSTRUCTIONS ACTUALLY ESTABLISH
Research begun 4 November; completed 5 November 2026

ASSESSMENT

The spatial argument survives in a substantial, bounded form. The Shroud has
organized tonal structure that can be interpreted as a body-to-cloth distance
pattern. Published work also demonstrates ways of fitting its frontal and
dorsal images to an anatomically plausible body with cloth-drape corrections.
These are real findings. Comparing the Shroud with a stylized medieval
painting does not dispose of them.

The reviewed work does not establish a uniquely recoverable, anatomically
perfect 3D scan. The impressive reconstructions use assumed cloth surfaces,
selected intensity-to-distance functions, anatomical reference bodies,
registration, and sometimes iterative sculptural fitting. These choices are
part of the scientific model, not additional measurements of the lost body.
Their use does not make the models worthless or automatically circular.
It does limit which conclusions the successful fits establish.

An important experimental counterexample exists: Emily Craig and Randall
Bresee produced a drawn face whose reported height-tone correlation was
similar in magnitude to the original Shroud statistic. Giulio Fanti and Mario
Moroni subsequently treated that technique as a particularly good 3D result,
while maintaining objections to its overall image-formation account. This
narrows a claim of unachievable artistic depth encoding. It does not establish
the Shroud's actual manufacture or reproduce its entire combination.

A useful newer contribution is Mario Latendresse's 2026 real-body/real-cloth
scan experiment. It strengthens the objection to declaring a body-based
image geometrically impossible on the basis of one simulated drape. It still
assumes a distance-dependent, vertically directed image process, and does
not demonstrate the physical cause of that process.

1. WHAT THE ORIGINAL 1984 STUDY MEASURED [S01]

John P. Jackson was at Kaman Sciences, Eric J. Jumper at the Air Force
Institute of Technology, and William R. Ercoline at the US Air Force Academy.
Their Applied Optics paper separates a small quantitative test from broader
visual/physical reconstruction work.

They measured the transmittance of a 1978 Vernon Miller photograph at
13 identifiable facial locations. They compared it with vertical gaps
measured between an approximately Shroud-like linen cloth and a bearded
volunteer, using side photographs and contour gauges.

Reported numerical results, printed pp. 2246-2247:

  Shroud: 13 locations; r-squared = 0.60.
  Reported 95% interval for population rho-squared: 0.13 to 0.86.
  Scatter expressed as equivalent distance: about 0.6 cm.
  Estimated body/drape uncertainty: approximately +/-0.4 cm.
  Estimated cloth-background contribution: approximately 0.2 cm.
  Fitted discoloration range to background: approximately 3.7 cm.

The authors also give an error-adjusted estimate: after assigning part of
the scatter to body/drape and background variation and assuming independent
errors, they infer r-squared around 0.86 and a population interval 0.59-0.96.
That is a conditional error decomposition, not a second direct measurement
of the Shroud or 13 additional data points.

Controls were informative. An ordinary photograph of a plaster face gave
r-squared 0.17, whereas an artificially distance-encoded phosphorescent
face gave 0.98. The Shroud was substantially more distance-like than their
ordinary photographic control. It was not numerically indistinguishable
from their near-ideal distance-encoded control.

This supports a relationship in that sampled comparison. The measurement
is not a 60% probability of authenticity, nor does 13 points validate every
pixel. The authors explicitly discuss the small sample and difficulty of
accurately registering many independent points. They estimate roughly
1,700 samples would be required for the particular precision target they
consider. Counting the VP-8 display pixels does not automatically supply
those independently registered observations.

2. THE ORIGINAL FULL-BODY MODEL WAS CORRECTED [S01]

The VP-8 converts image tone into height. Unlike arbitrary bright/dark
noise, the Shroud produces recognizable local anatomical relief. However,
the authors report that relationships over larger body distances were
incorrect: the nose region and hand region could each look plausible while
their heights relative to one another did not.

They built a cardboard model from 570 lateral profiles. At 18 longitudinal
locations they calculated a reference-surface correction using a volunteer's
body; the other 552 profiles were interpolated. This produces the corrected
model in Figure 8B, as distinct from the raw relief in Figure 8A.

That correction changes broad shape while preserving finer facial/finger
structure. The inferred reference surface resembles a draping cloth, which
is an affirmative consistency result. The authors' position is stronger
than merely observing a face in a height map.

Nevertheless the final body is not wholly independent anatomical information
read directly from tone. The volunteer supplied the broad reference shape.
The paper itself distinguishes a physically meaningful body/cloth account
from a convenient 3D description if another process made the image.

The original paper also allowed that a hot bas-relief could account for
distance correlation, resolution and similar chemistry while failing other
constraints. Its conclusion was about the combined requirements, not the
impossibility of generating any depth-like result artificially.

Visual check: figures/relief-figure8-correct-14.png, printed p. 2257.
Figure 8A is raw relief; 8B includes the reference-surface correction;
8C compares both with a volunteer. Figures 9-10 show the inferred surface
and an actual cloth drape.

3. JACKSON'S 1989 FOLLOW-UP IS SUBSTANTIVE [S02]

Jackson's Shroud Spectrum International paper compares vertical mapping
with mapping perpendicular to the cloth, and discusses body-normal mapping.
Under a common tone-distance relation, vertical mapping gives a more
anatomically plausible result. His Appendix B compares measurements with
a retained set of 50 volunteer photographs and includes image-boundary
uncertainty. This is more than an unsupported visual assertion.

The qualifications matter. On printed p. 12 he states that the assumed cloth
over the face had to be flatter than the actual experimental drapes to
produce reasonable facial height. He proposes a chin band or another factor
as a possible explanation. The body-normal case was not fully computed.
Table 4 also retains individual departures; a satisfactory mean does not
make every body measurement perfect.

The defensible outcome is an internally consistent first-order geometric
model that favors one mapping among the tested options. It does not identify
a physical energy source or establish that every alternative drape fails.

4. DOES THE FLAT IMAGE HAVE PERFECT PROPORTIONS? [S03, S02]

Ercoline and Robert C. Downs Jr. of the US Air Force Academy, with Jackson
of the University of Colorado at Colorado Springs, explicitly studied image
distortions in their 1982 IEEE conference paper. They report seven of
23 measurements as about two standard deviations or more from their
comparison means. The stretched widths, arm and finger dimensions were
interpreted as potentially consistent with cloth drape.

That challenges literal perfection of the flat image. It does not refute
Daniel's more careful proposition: a distorted flat image can still fit a
plausible body when the cloth is appropriately draped.

Some landmarks, notably a burned-away elbow, were estimated. The measurements
are correlated, and the reference population is not the unknown individual's
actual anatomy. It would be inappropriate to multiply their deviations into
an authenticity probability. Jackson's 1989 revision uses ten measurements,
explicit combined uncertainties and a different presentation; it should not
be silently treated as an identical repeat of the 1982 table.

5. PADUA'S 2010 AND 2018 RECONSTRUCTIONS [S04, S05]

Giulio Fanti, R. Basso and G. Bianchini of the University of Padua's
Mechanical Engineering Department used an adjustable Poser manikin in 2010.
They varied body dimensions, joints and cloth placement to align predefined
anthropometric landmarks. They reported compatibility between a 1.95 m
frontal image and 2.02 m dorsal image with a man 175 +/-2 cm tall. A half-scale
statue and printed cloth copy provided a physical wrapping check.

This is affirmative evidence that selected frontal/dorsal constraints can be
reconciled. The anatomical model was selected and resized; missing areas
were reconstructed iteratively. The reported +/-2 cm compatibility is not
a demonstration of +/-2 cm accuracy at every point of the original body.
The paper acknowledges approximately 1 cm uncertainty defining soft-tissue
landmarks and distinguishes that from coordinate-reading precision.

In 2018, Padua physician Matteo Bevilacqua, engineers/clinicians Gianmaria
and Stefano Concheri, Fanti and sculptor Sergio Rodella described repeated
adjustment of an iron skeleton, modeling clay and a Shroud copy until
selected correspondences were of order 1 cm. They explicitly state that the
intensity-distance relationship had been detected but not quantified. Their
standard anatomical reference and iterative adjustments supplied information
alongside the image. The result is a constrained reconstruction, not a
complete body recovered solely from calibrated image intensity.

Their approximately 180 cm model using the 2002 photograph is discussed in
terms of cloth stretching; they estimate roughly 177 cm before 2002. This
does not by itself disprove the earlier 175 +/-2 cm reconstruction.

6. TIGHT WRAPPING, LOOSE WRAPPING AND CT OVERLAYS [S06, S07]

The same Padua/Rodella group reported preliminary 2017 face-wrapping scans
and numerical work. The institutional abstract describes a tighter wrapping
than earlier hypotheses; the 2018 report gives a maximum face-cloth gap of
approximately 10 mm. That should not be equated with Jackson's approximately
3.7 cm fitted range: one describes a selected model's geometry; the other
is an extrapolation of tone versus distance. Their different assumptions
need reconciliation before claiming a unique cloth configuration.

Pierluigi Baima Bollone and Turin colleagues Giovanni Gandini and Paolo
Fonio, with Filippo Marchisio of ASL TO3 radiology, describe a CT overlay
using a selected volunteer with similar physical characteristics. This is
another anatomical compatibility approach. Only the publisher abstract was
available here; it does not establish an independently recovered CT scan
of the Shroud man. No full-text conclusions have been attributed to it.

7. AN EXPERIMENTAL COUNTERPART TO THE DEPTH CLAIM [S08, S09]

Forensic anthropologist Emily A. Craig and University of Tennessee textile
scientist Randall R. Bresee's 1994 paper deliberately uses dust buildup to
represent relief. Their 33-feature comparison reports r-squared 0.59 for
a drawn manikin face, versus 0.19 for its photograph. The 0.59 result concerns
the drawing; the paper also shows qualitative relief after transfer to linen.

This is a concrete artistic depth-encoding demonstration, not just a reminder
that any image can be extruded. But 0.59 versus the Shroud's 0.60 is not
an equivalence test: different landmarks, subjects and reference distances
were used, and height above a plane differs from a cloth-body gap. It does
not prove a full-body, microscopic or blood-pattern match.

Fanti of Padua and Mario Moroni's 2002 peer-reviewed comparison is especially
relevant. They identify Craig's technique as yielding perhaps the best 3D
results among their comparisons while retaining open formation questions.
Other contact experiments fare worse in luminance saturation and superficiality.
Their analysis is not an endorsement of a complete pigment manufacture.

The source reprint's introductory header incorrectly gives volume 34;
the bibliographic record and contemporary reprint identify JIST 38(1):59-67.

8. THE MORAES MODEL DOES NOT SETTLE ORIGIN [S10, S11, S12]

Arc-Team Brazil 3D researcher Cicero Moraes compares a body with a reduced-
depth relief using a Shroud-fitted digital model, default generic cotton
cloth settings and distance/contact visualization. A relief yields less
distorted contours in those simulations. Chemistry, blood behavior and
the complete original object are outside the study's test.

Tristan Casabianca, Emanuela Marinelli of Collegamento Pro Sindone and
Alessandro Piana published a 2026 methodological objection. Moraes replies
that his scope is morphological deformation. I checked the full original
paper and its figures; only the publisher abstracts and reference lists
of the 2026 exchange were accessible. Neither abstract has been treated
as the full argument.

My inference: the simulation illustrates a difficulty for particular
contact/drape setups. It does not prove that every human-body configuration
fails, or demonstrate the actual microscopic image-making process. A body
whose geometry was adjusted to the Shroud is also not independent evidence
that a medieval maker possessed the same model.

9. A NEWER REAL-BODY / REAL-CLOTH TEST [S13, S14]

Independent researcher Mario Latendresse's June 2026 preprint uses a real
supine volunteer and draped linen scanned separately with an iPhone and
Scaniverse. His model assumes vertical projection and a selected nonlinear
distance-tone rule. It produces a recognizable image with modest broad
distortion. This is a useful counter to universal claims based on a single
simulated tight wrap.

The supplied script confirms downward ray casting, an adjustable distance
cutoff and exponent, followed by angle-based flattening and global rescaling
to preserve total area. I inspected that code; I did not execute Blender or
independently reproduce its numerical output. Exact total-area preservation
does not alone prove correct local distances or weave-coordinate recovery.
That is a mathematical limitation of the validation, not a claimed reviewer
finding. The author acknowledges incomplete facial fidelity.

The archived PDF is labeled a preprint; its data README claims journal
publication, which I could not independently confirm in this pass.
Peer-reviewed status is therefore unverified here. Paper, code and data
metadata are saved. Image TIFFs, meshes and generated-image archives are
listed in the metadata but were not downloaded or independently validated.

10. AN IMPORTANT DISAGREEMENT ABOUT WHAT TO MEASURE [S02, S04, S13]

The following are author-reported definitions, not interchangeable measurements:

  Jackson 1989: frontal head-to-foot blood mark about 193.0 cm;
                dorsal head-to-top-of-heel about 177.8 cm.
  Padua 2010: front image 195 cm; back image 202 cm;
              inferred standing body height 175 +/-2 cm.
  Latendresse 2026: frontal image about 196 cm; dorsal about 188 cm;
                   his volunteer/model about 170 cm.

Endpoints and posture assumptions differ. The published headline body/image
lengths should not be averaged, or treated as several precise independent
measurements of the same quantity. A common landmark atlas and photograph
registration would materially improve this discussion. It is not justified
to resolve the disagreement simply by choosing the most impressive statue.

11. HOLOGRAMS AND PROCESSED FACES [S15, S16]

Giovanni Tamburelli of CSELT/University of Turin distinguishes original-image
reliefs from his processed woundless-face reconstructions. Removing a wound
does not create independent observations of the obscured original anatomy.
The latter outputs need their own validation.

Petrus Soons's conference account explicitly describes converting enhanced
photographic gray levels into depth, repairing/softening regions without
information, and generating multiple views for a holographic print. The
hologram is an additional visualization of the interpreted photograph. It
is not an independent optical recording of the lost person from multiple
directions, and its existence does not show that the cloth itself is a
wave-interference hologram.

12. WHY A GOOD FIT DOES NOT IDENTIFY A UNIQUE BODY

In the simplest vertical model, let C be the cloth's height, B the body's
height, and D the gap. Then D = C - B. If tone determines D, recovering B
still requires C. A different physically admissible C can yield a different
B with the same gap pattern. This is an explanation of the inverse problem,
not a newly measured fact about the Shroud.

Physical drape, anatomy, blood registration, symmetry and the backside
can constrain the possibilities; Jackson's work rightly uses such tests.
But a choice of cloth and transfer function cannot be counted as though
they had been independently measured on the lost original configuration.

Negative-like tones and depth-like shading can also be consequences of the
same distance-dependent process. They remain observations to explain,
but their probabilities cannot automatically be multiplied as separate
unrelated accidents. Latendresse makes this connection explicitly.

13. EFFECT ON THE JENGA BLOCK AND TALLY

Sustained: meaningful spatial organization; nontrivial body/cloth-compatible
reconstructions; inadequacy of ordinary medieval painting examples as a
direct quantitative rebuttal; no complete manufacture established here.

Narrowed: a perfect, unique 3D scan recovered from raw tone; treating a fitted
statue's 1 cm correspondence as independent whole-body depth accuracy.

Counterevidence to a narrower impossibility claim: experimentally drawn
depth-like shading, acknowledged as a good partial result by Fanti/Moroni.

Not established: that Craig's drawing, Garlaschelli's reproduction, Moraes's
digital relief or any other reviewed process explains the complete Shroud.
Conversely no reviewed geometry study dates the cloth, identifies Jesus,
or establishes Resurrection as the image's physical cause.

This audit therefore does not remove the whole spatial Jenga block. It
separates a sustainable body-compatibility argument from stronger uniqueness
and impossibility claims. This is the analyst's assessment, not a concession
attributed to Daniel. The existing modern-miracle numerical tally is unchanged.

The most informative next comparison would use the same source photograph,
fixed landmarks, explicit cloth measurements, multiple volunteers and
artificial controls, then test predictions at landmarks not used for fitting.
That would tell us much more than another attractive fitted head.

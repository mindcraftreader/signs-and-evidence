Wounds, infection and scar assessment — 28 October 2026

General medical literature:
S. Guo and L. A. DiPietro, University of Illinois at Chicago Center for Wound Healing and Tissue Regeneration, Factors Affecting Wound Healing (2010), DOI 10.1177/0022034509359125. Distinguishes colonization from infection; inflammation is a normal healing phase and can become prolonged in chronic wounds. No visible pus or erythema is not evidence of absence of all cellular inflammation.
Clement D. Marshall et al., Stanford University School of Medicine Division of Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery, Cutaneous Scarring: Basic Science, Current Treatments, and Future Directions (2018), DOI 10.1089/wound.2016.0696. Adult dermal repair generally produces scar rather than restoration of original normal skin architecture. This does not mean every superficial injury leaves an obvious visible scar.

Historical testimony:
Giuseppe Sala, physician and University of Naples medical semiotics assistant per editorial biographical note, statement dated 7 July 1969, Di Flumeri collection printed pp. 309–312 (PDF pages 156–158). Describes no circumscribed inflammation or purulent secretion, but does describe edema of the hands and feet. Reports feet changing several months before death, hands changing near death, and a final left-palm crust during the brief agony. Reports normal intact skin without visible scars ten minutes after death. He explicitly had not conducted a direct inspection of the living wounds; depth claims must therefore be evaluated against the earlier examiners, who disagreed. His postmortem observation is still positive testimony and should not be dismissed because of this limitation.

Assessment:
The full proposed pattern of deep persistent wounds, minimal visible inflammatory changes, no clinical infection, and scarless disappearance would be highly anomalous relative to ordinary adult wound healing. Neither merely calling the wounds chronic nor naming chemical irritation explains the whole pattern. However, no clinical infection is not the same as a sterile wound; no visible inflammation is not the same as no microscopic inflammation; and no visible scar is not histologically demonstrated regeneration. Records describe gradual fading of some sites, not simultaneous closure of all five in the last few days. Do not present daily screwdriver injury as an established requirement of all artificial-maintenance hypotheses.

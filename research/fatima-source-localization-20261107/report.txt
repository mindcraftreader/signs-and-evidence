F-06 | FÁTIMA | DID THE DISTANT WITNESSES LOCATE THE SOURCE?
7 November 2026

Result
The directional-convergence argument does not establish a light source a few
kilometres south of Cova da Iria. Figure 8 draws directions to the Cova and
places its proposed source separately to the south. The witness wording
does not supply the additional directions or elevations needed to identify
that position. A local emitter remains compatible with portions of the
record. This is a result about the published localization argument, not
about whether the witnesses saw something or the cause of the announced sign.

1. What Dalleur and Muse claim
Dalleur, 2021, pp. 27–30, interprets four distant groups as seeing the same
event in the Fátima direction and proposes a source over hills a few
kilometres south of the Cova. The Figure 8 caption calls the marker an
estimate. Muse, Our Lady of Fatima §2.2.2, goes further in calling the
bearing convergence evidence for a localized emitter due south. He treats
the anomalous D115 shadow elevation and the inferred geographic location
as separate lines of reasoning. In his reply to Scott Alexander, Muse
also argues from how the display varied by distance and viewing conditions.
The audit grants the D115 25–32° elevation and uses 28.24° as a numerical
example. The published photo azimuth is camera-relative. For the main
sensitivity test, a due-south geographic direction is granted as well.

2. What the diagram actually draws
I visually checked the original PDF pp. 27–29. The Moel, Leiria and
Alburitel lines end at 0, the Cova. The Minde line also goes to 0 and passes
the vicinity of the separate source symbol. The markers in a 155 dpi
render are approximately y=317 at the Cova, y=389 at the estimated source
and y=454 at Minde. Reading the 12.2 km Cova–Minde scale on that segment
puts the symbol roughly halfway, at about 6.4 km. The map is schematic.
This is a sensitivity location, not a geodetic estimate attributed to
Dalleur. Mark Grant's separate example uses 3 km due south. Both are tested.

Recomputed WGS84 geographic directions from the archive's observer
representatives, in degrees clockwise from true north:

  Site                    To the Cova    To 3 km south    To 6.4 km south
  São Pedro de Moel         114.1           118.6             123.2
  Leiria                    137.2           143.3             148.6
  Alburitel                 261.8           249.6             237.8
  Minde vicinity              5.7             7.4              11.2

For Moel, Leiria and Alburitel, the absolute offsets from the Sun's
near-noon south bearing to the Cova directions are 65.9°, 42.8° and 81.8°.
These closely reproduce Figure 8's labeled 65.7°, 42.5° and 82°. The
Figure 8 Minde offset is 179°. The town representative used here differs
by several degrees and the 1917 field is unidentified. The calculation
shows the map's inputs are consistent with town-to-Cova lines. It does
not treat the discrepancies as witness errors. If taken as precise lines
they miss the proposed source. If treated as broad directions, the text
does not say how much angular deviation is acceptable.

3. Witnesses and the best case for each
Minde. Haffert's pp. 40–41 interview is the strongest direction record.
Albano Barros was tending sheep near Minde and recalls the fall toward
Fátima. Fátima really is roughly north from the representative site,
opposite the midday Sun. This is significant positive evidence against
ordinary fixation on the midday Sun as an explanation of his description.
The statement gives no village, field, angular elevation or exact time.
The text about almost everyone fearing the end appears in Haffert's
p. 79 discussion without quotation marks. It supplies no second station.
On the WGS84 great circle between the Minde proxy and the Cova, points
1, 3, 6.4 and 8 km out from the Cova all have the same bearing from the
Minde proxy. Matching the D115 angle then requires heights of approximately
0.89, 1.96, 3.79 and 4.65 km above sea level. This construction assigns the
Cova bearing roughly 186°, and a separate run grants 180° exactly. On the
due-south track the Minde bearings vary by about 0.5–9.2° from the Cova
direction over that range. These are demonstrations of range ambiguity.
They are not evidence for any one of the modeled heights or distances.

Leiria. Haffert's p. 41 interview has Guilhermina Lopes da Silva looking
toward a mountain and describing a red flash in the sky at noon. She
reports calling two men. Their own directional statements are unavailable.
The address is introduced as where she lives at the time of publication.
The author's explanation on pp. 41–42 invokes the terrain tentatively.
Dalleur attributes the masking to da Silva and computes a horizon from
the address. The text does not establish the 1917 building, floor,
window, mountain, or elevation of the flash. A 2023 municipal record
verifies the current street and house numbering, without tying it to
the observation. The 1918–19 city plan is known. No named 1917 occupancy
or observation record was recovered.

To check Muse's terrain interpretation without supplying a witness
address, I sampled a modern Copernicus GLO-30 surface model from the
Leiria locality and the government coordinates for the Encarnação hill,
at 1.7 and 5 m above each sampled surface. Along the target directions,
the highest surface beyond 2 km was about 1.6–2.3° above horizontal.
For the hill proxy, a source 3 km south appears at about 5.7° and a source
6.4 km south at about 9.8°. Both clear the modeled distant terrain. Near
obstructions in the lower city proxy are much more sensitive, up to about
8° in the sampled model. Modern trees and buildings are part of the
surface product. These are conditional geometric comparisons with no
1917 cloud, building or exact witness reconstruction. A low ridge does
not set the red flash's elevation. Muse's selective-extinction idea is
physically compatible with a redder appearance over distance, but the
reported color does not supply a bearing, range, or spectrum.

Amorim's 1960 appendix p. 202 cites the 8 November 1917 O Mensageiro,
whose editor says people in Leiria saw the event without going to Fátima.
Ferraz independently identifies the 8 November issue as no. 161 and
points to DCF III-1 p. 617 for Lacerda's article. Muse dates the newspaper
to November 5. The original newspaper page was not recovered. This early
report is relevant to the geographic spread, but gives no witness bearing.

São Pedro de Moel. The house is identified by its municipal coordinates.
Vieira describes a surprising spectacle from his veranda and recalls
having forgotten the prediction. De Marchi p. 176 and Jaki's quotation
from the 1937 Marques da Cruz book, reporting a 1935 conversation, supply
no compass direction. In Brochado's account the widow herself observed
the spectacle and says they learned afterward of the Cova connection.
The Shrine's February 1978 Voz da Fátima reproduces this distinction.
Haffert's location over the mountain appears in his narration on p. 40.
He says the widow confirmed the experience in 1960. Her original
deposition was not recovered. The museum history identifies the historic
interior veranda as sea-facing. The modern photographs show an end
opening as well as sea-facing windows. This is a reason to require a
specific viewing position before using the architecture as a bearing.
It is not a basis to say the family could not see inland sky.

Alburitel. De Marchi gives Inácio's school narrative, which he says
he checked with the teacher and other villagers. Amorim pp. 202–204
prints Fonseca's account of what Joaquim told her about the separate
hilltop group. Haffert attributes the school story to Joaquim and
places both brothers together. Dalleur also treats them together.
Muse's distinction between the two groups is supported by the
Portuguese source chains. Both report descent and rotation. The school
story calls what people pointed at the sun and gives no azimuth. The
hill story describes a wide horizon including the Fátima ridges, and
gives no explicit bearing linking the disc to those ridges. Neither
station is recovered closely enough to use as an angular measurement.

4. Check against Muse's more distant corroboration
The Granja letter was written the next day and relays apparent rotation
and a pink cloud passing in front of what people called the Sun. The
Oeiras account is a later secondhand report of what José Leite saw.
Neither gives a bearing or angular size. Their retention as evidence of
geographic spread is separate from treating them as sightings of the
same compact physical disc.

I reproduced Mark Grant's scale check under his assumption of a disc
subtending about 0.53° from the Cova. That is an assumption based on the
solar comparison, not a measured historical diameter. For a source
3 km south and at a 28.24° Cova elevation, the disc appears about
0.016° at Oeiras and 0.011° at Granja. Elevations for a coastal eye at
2 m are about 0.45° and −0.01° with no refraction, or 0.52° and 0.09°
with a conventional illustrative 7/6 effective Earth radius. This is
an unobstructed geometric calculation, without local terrain or measured
atmospheric conditions. With 6.4 km distance the apparent diameters are
about 0.034° and 0.024°, still much smaller than the solar disc. Muse's
diffuse reddening idea can explain why a flash could be seen across
distance without preserving a resolved rotating disc. It supplies no
quantitative model that predicts which features would be discernible
from the two beaches. This is a tension for that single compact-disc
interpretation, not an exclusion of detecting a sufficiently bright light.

5. Adjudication
The evidence supports several separate lines of testimony, including a
particularly discriminating Minde report. It does not provide the
independent crossing directions used to locate the emitter in Muse's
argument. D115's angular evidence has been held fixed in this test. The
specific published localization inference is not sustained. The dialogue
score records the component review separately because Daniel authorized
the audit without adopting the map location as a premise of his case.

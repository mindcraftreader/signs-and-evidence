The Shroud of TurinHistory, stories, and Fr. Andrew Dalton’s references

The identifiable cloth’s secure history begins in medieval Lirey. Earlier Christian image traditions are real historical subjects; identifying their objects with the Turin Shroud is the disputed step.

Documented history

“Secure history” means evidence for the object or its custody, reception, and investigation. It does not by itself decide who made the image or whose body it represents. Even sources favorable to authenticity distinguish the uncertain early period from the mid-fourteenth-century record. [CISS] [CISS-D]

 | c. 1350s | Lirey, Champagne

The cloth identifiable with today’s Turin Shroud is exhibited in the circle of Geoffroi de Charny and Jeanne de Vergy. Dalton gives 1354; use “mid-fourteenth century” or “c. 1355” unless discussing a particular document. The founding of the church in 1353 is not itself a dated certificate for the cloth. [N12] [CISS] | Secure general period; exact first exhibition debated

 | 1355–1382 | Nicole Oresme’s testimony

A treatise dated to this interval calls the Shroud an example of clerical fraud. The passage’s modern publication in 2025 adds evidence about its early reception; it is not a newly discovered maker’s confession. [SAR25] | Medieval accusation; date is a range

 | 1389–1390 | The Lirey controversy

Bishop Pierre d’Arcis challenges the exhibitions and reports an earlier inquiry under Henri de Poitiers and a maker’s admission. Avignon claimant Clement VII permits display with language identifying a representation of Christ’s shroud. The documents establish a dispute; the alleged artisan is not independently available to examine. [N12] | Documented controversy

 | 1418; 1453 | Marguerite de Charny → Savoy

The cloth leaves Lirey for safekeeping during war, is not returned to the canons, and is transferred to Louis of Savoy in 1453. The financial and legal character of the transfer deserves more care than “a simple gift.” [N12] [CISS] | Documented transfer history

 | 1502; 1506 | Chambéry and liturgical recognition

The Shroud is installed at the ducal chapel in Chambéry in 1502. Julius II authorizes its own feast in 1506, celebrated on 4 May. Public devotion also becomes part of Savoy dynastic ceremony. [N12] [COURT] | Documented cult and custody

 | 4 Dec. 1532; 1534 | Fire and repairs

Fire damages the folded cloth at Chambéry. Poor Clares patch it and add a backing in 1534. Invisible corner reweaving is a separate hypothesis. [CISS-H] | Documented damage and conservation; corner hypothesis separate

 | 1578 | Transfer to Turin

The move to Turin shortens Charles Borromeo’s pilgrimage to fulfill a post-plague vow and fits the relocation of the Savoy capital. [CISS-H] [N12] | Documented move with devotional and political setting

 | 1694 | Guarini chapel

It is installed in the chapel joining cathedral and royal palace. [CISS-H] | Documented installation

 | 28 May 1898 | Secondo Pia photographs the cloth

Official photography reveals its striking reversed-tone appearance and makes reproducible images widely available for study. [MET] | Documented photographic event

 | 1902; May 1931 | Delage and Enrie

Delage argues for authenticity and protests how his work is received. In 1931 Enrie produces a new, carefully documented photographic series. Enrie’s work confirms a photographic appearance, rather than dating the cloth. [DEL] [MET] | Documented scholarship and photography; anecdotes need context

 | 1939–1946 | Wartime refuge

The cloth shelters at Montevergine Abbey near Avellino during the war. [CISS-H] | Documented custody exception

 | 1978; 1983 | STURP and papal ownership

STURP carries out its major examination in 1978. On Umberto II’s death in 1983, ownership passes from Savoy to the Holy See; the Turin archbishop serves as custodian. [C14] [CISS] | Documented investigation and ownership

 | 1988–1989 | Radiocarbon dating

Three laboratories measure material from one strip in 1988; the 1989 Nature paper reports AD 1260–1390. Later objections concern statistics, sampling, or contamination; those objections are not themselves a newly measured first-century date. [C14] | Published measurement, with subsequent debate

Firefighters rescue the cloth in 1997. Conservation in 2002 removes old patches and replaces the backing. [CISS-H] | Documented rescue and intervention

 | 24 May 1998 | John Paul II’s address

The pope affirms its devotional value while leaving investigation of its historical and scientific questions to researchers. Veneration is not a declaration settling its age or identity. [JP2] | Primary papal statement

The lore and the stories behind the route

These are the principal stories in the saved interview plus the source traditions needed to understand them. “Not established” leaves a claim open; it does not mean no such tradition existed.

Scripture and interpretation

The burial and the empty tomb

The Gospels describe burial linens. Dalton reads John 20’s separate face cloth and lying linens as a sign of the Resurrection, and proposes wrappings that collapsed without being unbound. John does not describe a body image or identify the Turin object. His reading is theological exegesis; it cannot fill the later custody gap. [JOHN] [CCC]Dalton / context: 00:53:51–01:04:00

Developing late antique legend

Abgar’s letters and healing

Abgar, king of Edessa, asks Jesus for healing. In Eusebius, Jesus promises a disciple; Thomas sends Thaddeus, one of the Seventy, who heals the king. This differs from Dalton’s compressed Jude/one-of-the-Twelve-and-Shroud version. Eusebius supplies evidence for an early fourth-century tradition, not a first-century record of a Shroud image. [EUS] [N14]Dalton / context: 02:34:54–02:35:26; 02:40:44–02:41:14

Different recensions, not one fixed narrative

The painter becomes the miraculous image

In the Doctrine of Addai, the envoy Hannan paints Jesus with pigments. Later traditions make the cloth image occur miraculously when Jesus wipes his face, or connect it with the sweat of his Passion. These versions should be told separately. A portrait sent by a living Jesus is not automatically the burial sheet of a dead Jesus. [N14]Dalton / context: Context for 02:35:50–02:36:25

Legendary hiding and rediscovery

The image hidden above the city gate

One developed narrative has a pagan grandson threaten the image. A bishop seals it behind a tile with a burning lamp. Centuries later it is found, the lamp still burning, and the image has copied itself onto the tile, the Keramion. Dalton says “son” and gives a shorter account. The striking details belong to later narrative development. [GUS] [N14]Dalton / context: 02:35:26–02:35:50

Historical settings plus miraculous narrative

The 525 flood and the 544 siege

The flood at Edessa and the Persian siege are separate events. A later story makes rediscovery and the image’s protection of the city part of the siege narrative. Evagrius’s late sixth-century account includes an image that helps defeat the attackers; Procopius’s earlier account does not. Neither event dates the Turin linen directly. [N14] [GUS]Dalton / context: 02:35:40–02:35:50

Proposed material identification

Tetradiplon: the long sheet hidden in a small portrait

Wilson’s influential reconstruction folds the long Shroud so only the face shows, explaining descriptions of a towel or face image. Dalton uses the folding idea. Nicolotti examines the Greek word and the Acts of Thaddaeus in context: folding vocabulary does not require a concealed full-length corpse image, and the text distinguishes the face cloth from burial linens. It is an ingenious hypothesis, not a custody document. [N14] [WIL]Dalton / context: 01:19:40–01:20:15

History of the Mandylion; Turin identity disputed

944: an imperial acquisition and procession

The Edessan image is brought to Constantinople in 944 and receives ceremonial veneration. This transfer has stronger textual support than its legendary first-century origin. Whether it was the Turin Shroud remains a further identification. Avoid turning the well-attested movement of one named relic into proof of the identity of another. [N14] [CISS]Dalton / context: 02:36:29–02:36:48

Genuine medieval testimony with an identification problem

Byzantine burial cloths and Robert de Clari

Mesarites describes burial linens without explicitly describing a body image; Nicolotti stresses the plural and the theological rhetoric. Robert de Clari separately describes a miraculous face cloth/tile at the Pharos chapel and a shroud display at Blachernae. His shroud passage is a serious lead, but similarity and disappearance after the conquest do not establish a route to Lirey. Keep the distinct shrines and relic descriptions visible. [N14]Dalton / context: Background to 02:36:48–02:37:11; de Clari is not named in the saved captions

Competing reconstructions

1204–1350s: Crusaders, Templars, Athens, Languedoc

Dalton calls these the open or missing years and acknowledges disagreement. Proposed routes run through Crusader families and Greece, through the Templars, or through southern France. Markwardt supplies a Languedoc hypothesis and a separate early-history reconstruction involving Antioch. More proposed routes do not add up to one established itinerary. Do not confuse the Templar Geoffroi de Charney with the Lirey knight Geoffroi de Charny on the basis of similar names. [MK-L] [MK14] [N14]Dalton / context: 02:37:04–02:39:14

Old objects; proposed dependence

Sinai icons, facial details, and the Pray Codex

Dalton compares the Sinai Pantocrator and later images with the Shroud, including unusual facial marks. His spoken “Paulino” is an uncertain identification; Paul Vignon is a plausible research lead, not a confirmed caption correction. The Pray manuscript is securely late twelfth century. His argument from concealed thumbs, diagonal pattern, and L-shaped dots is a proposed identification of what its artist depicted. It does not follow simply from dating the book. [PRAY] [N14]Dalton / context: 00:08:25–00:08:38; 02:16:32–02:21:22; 02:30:03–02:34:13

Separate relic and a conditional comparison

A second cloth: the Sudarium of Oviedo

Dalton argues that matching stains and an early history for the Sudarium would anchor the Shroud earlier. Both premises need checking. He himself hesitates over AD 611. The Oviedo teaching source takes the 1075 opening of the Arca Santa as its historical starting point. The proposed earlier journey, the recorded cult, bloodstain interpretation, and same-person inference should not be compressed into one proved fact. [SUD] [SUD-O]Dalton / context: 00:33:00–00:34:52; 02:34:21–02:34:39

Material evidence of devotion

The badge carried home by a Lirey pilgrim

The small surviving badge depicts an exhibition and associates it with Lirey. It makes a vivid teaching story about medieval pilgrims taking an image home. Catalog details require care: the French museum record calls it lead/tin and dates it before 1453 in the first half of the fifteenth century, while popular summaries often call it bronze and date it to the 1350s. It supports the Lirey cult without settling its exact first year. [BADGE] [CISS]Dalton / context: 00:08:00–00:08:22

Documented event plus modern explanatory hypothesis

The fire, the nuns, and the invisible repair

Alongside the documented fire repairs, Dalton tells the modern story of Sue Benford and Raymond Rogers: a dismissed repair theory supposedly prompts Rogers to inspect fibers and change his mind. His “five minutes” anecdote is an account of a conversation; the relevant evidence is the published material analysis. Known patching is not proof that the carbon-dated strip was invisibly replaced. [CISS-H]Dalton / context: 00:35:29–00:39:38; 01:19:40–01:20:36; 02:27:00–02:29:15

Modern discovery story, checked against records

Pia’s darkroom and Delage’s dissent

Pia’s first photographs in 1898 made a clearer human face visible in the negative. Doubts about those photographs led to further photography; Enrie’s 1931 series is independently documented. Dalton then recounts Delage’s identification of Jesus and opposition to publication. Delage’s relevant intervention was in 1902, so do not narrate it as occurring after Enrie’s 1931 photographs simply because Dalton tells the episodes in that order. [MET] [DEL]Dalton / context: 00:24:25–00:29:11

Documented devotional and dynastic culture

Copies, royal ceremonies, and the journey to Turin

Dalton describes copies made sacred by touching the cloth and the Savoy family’s use of displays on major occasions. The academic literature studies those practices as part of the relic’s social history. The documented journey in the timeline also connects pilgrimage with the court’s movements. [COURT] [CISS-H] [N12]Dalton / context: 00:24:46–00:24:59; 02:20:27–02:21:22

Attributed pastoral testimony

Healing and conversion stories in the interview

Asked about confirmed miracles, Dalton returns to Abgar but says he does not know whether the leprosy healing truly occurred. He instead tells of a skeptical Spanish schoolgirl whose response changed after an exhibition visit. This is his pastoral anecdote, not a medically documented cure or independent proof of the relic’s age. Dalton / context: 02:40:44–02:43:25

Six distinctions to preserve when telling this story

The date of a surviving text and the date of the event it narrates are different.

An ancient reference to a cloth or portrait does not alone identify the Turin object.

The Edessan face image, Byzantine burial linens, Veronica traditions, and the Sudarium of Oviedo require separate identification and custody arguments.

A medieval accusation of fabrication is valuable evidence about reception; the alleged maker’s independent testimony has not survived in the material reviewed here.

The Pray Codex’s date is established independently of the disputed identification of its painted textile.

Neither an unexplained image nor an objection to a dating method proves a Resurrection mechanism or a first-century age.

Bibliography and what was actually read

The access labels prevent a catalog entry or abstract from masquerading as a full-text review. This dossier is a substantial research pass, not a claim to have exhausted every archive or publication.

JOHN · Primary text consulted [https://bible.usccb.org/bible/john/20] Gospel of John, chapter 20 (USCCB)

Primary scriptural text: linens, separate head cloth, seeing and believing. No body image or later Turin provenance is specified.

CCC · Primary doctrinal text consulted [https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P1S.HTM] Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraph 640

Connects the condition of the empty tomb and its cloths with recognition of the Resurrection; does not identify the Turin Shroud or describe an image-formation mechanism.

N14 · Selected full chapters consulted [https://ebookcentral.proquest.com/lib/uakron/detail.action?docID=1815755] Andrea Nicolotti, From the Mandylion of Edessa to the Shroud of Turin (Brill, 2014)

University of Akron → ZipSearch → Ebook Central. Chapters 2 (pp. 7–28), 3 (pp. 29–52), and 5 (pp. 89–119) downloaded through Chrome and consulted; 77 of the permitted 91 PDF pages. These chapters examine the textual traditions rather than assuming the Turin identification.

N12 · Full text consulted [https://iris.unito.it/bitstream/2318/1659750/5/Nicolotti%2C%20Les%20tribulations%20d%27un%20linceul%20%28L%27histoire%29.pdf] Andrea Nicolotti, “Les tribulations d’un linceul,” L’Histoire 372 (February 2012), pp. 40–45

Previously saved full PDF consulted for the Lirey controversy, other shrouds, Savoy history, and the distinction between an image and a burial cloth.

CISS · Full webpage consulted [https://www.sindone.it/en/storia-1] International Centre of Sindonology, History

Institutional overview: secure fourteenth-century beginning, proposed earlier links, 1453 transfer, 1983 bequest. Read its hypotheses as hypotheses even where a narrative becomes more assertive.

CISS-H · Full text consulted [https://www.sindone.it/en/_files/ugd/6a2f2b_2d80c25072f34c59b6119a6ee7461198.pdf?index=true] International Centre of Sindonology, Le ipotesi storiche

Three-page institutional chronology, including conservation and custody.

CISS-D · Opening historical framework consulted [https://www.sindone.it/en/_files/ugd/6a2f2b_76a973401725455da61ac34d6a0a8f05.pdf] International Centre of Sindonology, Santa Sindone: immagine e reliquia

Six-page history of devotion: distinguishes tolerated, accepted, promoted cult and the period of scientific debate. Makes the absence of secure identification before the fourteenth century explicit.

EUS · Primary text consulted in translation [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250101.htm] Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History I.13

Early fourth-century account of Abgar’s correspondence and healing by Thaddeus, one of the Seventy, sent by Thomas. No image or Turin cloth appears in this chapter.

GUS · Selected full-text passages consulted [https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/24278014/MARK_GUSCIN_PhD_THESIS_05.03.15.pdf] Mark Guscin, The Tradition of the Image of Edessa, doctoral thesis (Royal Holloway, 2014; deposited 2015)

Especially pp. 28–30, 52–54, 103–105, and the chronological tables. Useful for different legend recensions, the lamp/tile story, the 544 siege, and the limits of the evidence after 1204. Relevant passages consulted, not the entire 424-page thesis.

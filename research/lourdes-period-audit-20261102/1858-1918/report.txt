LOURDES HEALING CLAIMS, RECOVERIES1858–1918
2 November 2026

RESULT

The strongest new finding is access to original-era medical publications rather
than another set of devotional summaries. The 1899 joint De Rudder paper has been
retrieved in full scan, along with Le Bec's 1922 medical case collection and
Boissarie's 1900 book. Canonical BMJ1910 page images reproduce treating-doctor and
examining-doctor testimony about Marie Borel. Actual published photographs show
De Rudder's healed bones and Borel's recently closed fistula sites.

These documents substantially improve provenance. They are not the complete raw
clinical files, and their publication does not mean that a journal independently
certified supernatural causation. No new miracle verdict or tally point is assigned.

USER'S QUESTION PRESERVED

The hypothesis under examination is a recurring, religiously identified place or
practice of healing associated with Mary's apparition and Lourdes. An unexplained
individual recovery alone is not automatically a vindication of a religion. Exact
context matters: French Lourdes baths, water taken home, Eucharistic procession,
replica shrine and distant prayer are recorded separately. Several cases commonly
included in the Lourdes corpus did not occur in the French baths.

PERIOD COVERAGE

The inherited official 72-case summary list contains 40 recoveries in 1858–1918,
all dated no later than 1911:1850s 7; 1860s 0; 1870s 2; 1880s 3; 1890s 13; 1900s 13;
1910–1918:2. These are officially recognized cases in that current list, not all
reported cures or all medical investigations. Zero in a decade does not establish
zero claims. The parent audit handles wider denominators.

This pass has 12 targeted candidate entries, including unrecognized candidates,
and prioritizes four below. It does not claim to have medically re-examined all
40 cases. Source IDs refer to source-register.json; cases have exact URLs, dates,
reading limits and next-document targets in case-register.json.

1. PIERRE DE RUDDER —7 APRIL1875; RECOGNIZED25 JULY1908

Priority: highest for an unusually substantial physical/documentary evidence chain.
Context: prayer/pilgrimage at Oostakker's Lourdes-replica grotto, Belgium, not the
French Lourdes pool. The 1899 narrative describes drinking local fountain water;
the official summary says no connection with water from the French grotto.

The original paper is L.Van Hoestenberghe, E.Royer and A.Deschamps, S.J.,
Revue des questions scientifiques 46(series 2,16), October 1899, pp 517–560[E01].
Van Hoestenberghe was a local physician and examiner; Royer a physician conducting
the 1893 investigation; Deschamps a Jesuit doctor of medicine/natural sciences.
This is an original-era scientific-journal publication, not a modern blinded trial.

Its facts section pp 517–529 and documentary appendix pp 555–560 contain named
witnesses and specific source dates. The authors reproduce a 27 April 1875 neighbors'
attestation concerning the exposed fracture before pilgrimage, and a 15 April 1875
statement of recovery signed by local officials and clergy. They cite a 9 April 1875
religious newspaper account and aJuly 1875 Scheerlinck book. These earlier issues
were not independently obtained in this pass; their transcription/provenance is
documented in the 1899 publication. Several more detailed interviews date 1893/1899.

Thus 'nothing was recorded until eighteen years afterward' would be inaccurate.
Conversely, every detailed sentence in 1899 cannot be treated as an independent
1875 record. One very detailed travel witness statement, for example, is expressly
signed 9 July 1899. The appendix distinguishes these dates instead of hiding them.

Exhumed bones demonstrate an old fracture and later union, with visible residual
deformity. Le Bec's actual plate was visually inspected[E02, PDFpage 147]. The
anatomical specimen supports the reality of structural injury; it cannot alone
date union to the pilgrimage day. The rapidity argument depends on the before/
after observational chain. No before-cure X-ray is claimed for 1867–75.

There is a concrete timing conflict requiring original records: the 1899 paper
places Van Hoestenberghe's detailed examinations in 1874 and late 1874. Dr Paul
Deschepper's 2019 FIAMC account says the physician later acknowledged only one
pre-cure examination in 1871, citing De Meester's 1957 documentary edition[E08].
This later article also describes bishop correspondence rediscovered in 1956.
Those letters need direct inspection. Deschepper's repeated 'eight years ten
months' conflicts with the event dates; the 1899 paper correctly says about eight
years two months. These discrepancies do not by themselves establish fabrication.

Useful next records: original 1875 bishop correspondence; Scheerlinck 1875/76;
De Meester 1957 full edition; original Logie/Deschamps 1903 medical exchange; surviving
specimens and dated radiographs assessed by an orthopedic specialist. The claim
that bandaging enabled gradual prior union must confront the actual recent
mobility/wound testimony, not merely the existence of healed bones.

2. MARIE BOREL —21–22 AUGUST1907; RECOGNIZED4 JUNE1911

Priority: strongest newly retrievable early case specifically connected to the
French Lourdes baths, with externally observable lesions and close clinical dates.

Le Bec, honorary surgeon at St Joseph's Hospital, Paris and Lourdes Bureau
president, presents a detailed account at printedpp 175–180[E02]. A real plate is
captioned 22 August 1907 and shows the six closed-site scars. We retrieved the full
scan and visually inspected the plate and key page 177. The old operation records
were not available even to Le Bec; he expressly says so.

The course is more specific than 'six holes disappeared instantaneously': four
outlets reportedly closed between morning and evening dressings on 21 August; the
other two on 22 August. On 23 August, the author reports Desplats/Sablé examination:
all tracks closed, but two sites still lacked complete epidermis, which developed
over subsequent days. This is still a claim of strikingly rapid organic recovery;
the exact staged description must replace an inflated instantaneous-regrowth story.

Thurston's 20 August 1910 BMJ article p 470[E06] reproduces a 15 October 1907 letter
from Dr Bardol of Mende, who personally operated/explored the patient and confirms
the fistula history and unexpected recovery. It also cites Dr Henri Guinier,
former agrégé libre of Montpellier's medical faculty, describing his own
22 August 1907 and 20 August 1908 examinations in Études 5 December 1909, p 595.

Guinier's account was published within two years of his examination, and the BMJ
reproduction within three. These are appreciably better than a present-day shrine
summary. Nevertheless, Herbert Thurston was a Jesuit author who explicitly calls
himself medically a layman. This is documentation published in BMJ, not BMJ
independently undertaking a clinical validation or declaring a miracle.

Actual limits: no before photograph, complete operative report, fistulography,
microbiology or complete daily treatment/nutrition chart was obtained. Tuberculosis
is inferred in Le Bec, not established by a recovered culture or histology.
Six external outlets should not silently become six independent full-thickness
intestinal perforations; the account describes communicating fistulae. The BMJ
account associates Desplats/Sablé observations with 22 August, Le Bec with 23 August.
The Italian official narrative has an apparent 1919/1907 typo, preserved in register.

A genuine ordinary medical comparator is Visschers et al., Maastricht University
Medical Centre surgery/NUTRIM,2012[E11]. Their prospective 79-patient cohort had
23 spontaneous closures, median 39 days, range 7–163, under sepsis/nutrition/wound care.
This refutes a categorical assertion that intestinal fistulae never close without
an operation. It does not reproduce Borel's reported overnight multi-outlet closure
after a chronic course. The clock in that study runs from convalescence/treatment,
not from the last observed discharge, so it is not a calibrated bound on the final
closure interval. No standard deviation, probability or matched diagnosis is claimed.

Next records: original 1907 Bureau file; Bardol's actual signed letter; Guinier's
complete 1909 article; nurses' notes; anatomical connections and treatment timeline.
Public modern criticism that only a shrine-recognized story exists is too broad;
primary-era testimony and photographs are public. Questions about completeness
and modern interpretation of that testimony remain substantive.

3. JOACHIME DEHANT —SEPTEMBER1878; RECOGNIZED25 APRIL1908

Priority: large externally visible wound with reproduced dated physician
certificates, more directly tied to the baths than De Rudder.

Le Bec 146–151[E02] reproduces Dr Gustave Froidebise's 6 September 1878 pre-journey
description and 19 Septemberpost-journey finding. He identifies an extensive
knee-to-ankle ulcer, discusses 32×15 cm dimensions, and describes dramatic closure
after baths. Names include treating Dr Manrique of Gesves. Froidebise is identified
as doctor at Ohey, Namur; a university professorship is not established here.

The source reports 16 nonfamily witnesses interviewed separately at home in 1893,
with written declarations and later favorable follow-up. That constitutes a
specific investigation, not 16 new observations independently collected by us.

Important discrepancy: the official summary dates healing 13 September 1878; Le Bec
says 15 September. No original signed certificates, photographs, microbiology or
original 1893 report was recovered. The fundamental lesion/closure claim is stronger
than further precise claims about destroyed tendon replacement or exact depth.

Next records: original 1878 certificates and Royer 1893 report, plus the 1908 diocesan
assessment. A date discrepancy is a reason to reconcile sources, not by itself a
disproof of the documented change.

4. MARIE LEBRANCHU —20 AUGUST1892; RECOGNIZED6 JUNE1908

Priority: possible bacteriological support and a public, controversial contemporary
setting around Zola's visit.

Boissarie 1900 pp 74–79[E03] quotes a Lourdes register entry describing cavitary
pulmonary tuberculosis and earlier characteristic bacilli in sputum at Hôtel-Dieu,
in Professor Germain Sée's service. It names hospice physician Marquezy's
certificate and describes negative post-bath auscultation and repeated follow-up.
Printed p 74 was visually checked; p 75 was rendered for review; the register quotation is expressly dated
22 August 1893. It is not the actual pre-cure hospital bacteriology sheet.

No paired radiographs or original lab slides/culture report were obtained.
Claims of immediate disappearance of structural cavities are inferred from
clinical examination, not before/after imaging. Zola's fictional Grivotte must not
be used as a literal independent clinical follow-up for the real patient.

Next records: Hôtel-Dieu and Franco-Dutch hospital records; 1892/93 Bureau entries;
actual sputum method/results and Zola's nonfiction observations. François(Lyon 1),
Sternberg(NIH)andFee(NLM)retrospective scholarly review[E10] preserves the case's
importance while highlighting variable early diagnostic standards and follow-up.

ADDITIONAL REGISTERED CANDIDATES

Marie Biré 1908: official summary says sight returned after Mass at the grotto;
same-day small-print reading despite residual optic-disc pallor, later resolution.
Original named ophthalmologist reports and fundus findings remain unretrieved.
Do not claim experimentally verified regrowth of destroyed optic nerves.

Gabrielle Durand 1908: Le Bec 140–146 describes his own treatment at St Joseph's,
a later chest film and normal peritoneum at an operation in 1916. This offers a
new first-person hospital source to investigate, though not a complete original
chart or pre-cure histology. Not in the current 72 recognized list.

Marguerite Verzier 1909: Le Bec 131–140 reproduces Dr Jean Tournaire's dated
certificate (Lyon medical faculty graduate, treating physician at Tain), with
7 Julyabnormal mobility and 9 Julywalking/apparent union; Professor Eugène Vincent
of Lyon/Charité examined her in 1911 and supplied later X-ray discussion. Vincent
explicitly cautions this was a 3-month-old delayed-union fracture, not a fresh
fracture healed instantly. Residual shortening/deformity remained. This occurred
at La Teppe during prayer/communion, not the French spring/pool. Not in 72 list.

Joseph Duncan Boothman 1910: BMJp 471 reproduces pre-event surgeon Richard Lake's
12 Mayletter and post-event C.F.Murphy, F.R.C.S.,2 Juneletter about an ear recovery
associated with Mass at Dover's Home of Our Lady of Lourdes. Actual otoscopic
images/audiometry are absent. Not a French-pool recovery or a recognized 72 case.

Louis Bouriette and Blaisette Cazenave 1858 remain early ocular-record targets.
Bouriette's old injury must be distinguished from the shorter reported complete
blindness interval. Marie Bailly 1902 and Gabriel Gargam 1901 are retained as
well-known unrecognized candidates with independent hospital/court leads, not
presented as newly completed case investigations. Absence from 72 is not proof of
ecclesiastical rejection.

WHAT THIS PASS HAS NOT ESTABLISHED

No raw original full clinical dossier obtained, no original preserved bones examined
by us, no physician personally consulted, no mail/contact/purchase undertaken, and
no numeric likelihood or broad supernatural/ordinary verdict computed. Long
medical history is real; its documentation changes across decades. A modern
diagnostic standard cannot be projected backward without examining the actual
tests, while an early date is not grounds to discard precise surviving evidence.

The four priority cases justify focused document requests, with Borel now an
especially practical early Lourdes-bath case. Parent should present the actual
published evidence and precise gaps together, not say either 'all merely anecdotal'
or 'fully independently established supernatural tissue regeneration'.

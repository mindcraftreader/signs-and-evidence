PADRE PIO, ELVIRA SERRITELLI AND CLEONICE MORCALDI
A feminist examination of the evidence and a register of dissenting voices
Research updated 31 October 2026

FINDING
The recovered material establishes that a serious sexual allegation entered the investigation. It does not establish that the affair happened. It also does not let us independently verify the full evidentiary reasons for rejecting it. There is a distinct, church-side account of Cleonice succeeding Elvira in devotional leadership. That succession must not be confused with proof of sexual succession.

This is an evidence-centered feminist reading, rather than an attribution of one verdict to all feminists. It asks who could speak, who recorded and translated their speech, whose motives were presumed, and whether credibility standards were applied consistently. Miranda Fricker, then a Birkbeck, University of London philosopher, explains prejudice-driven credibility deficits; University of Michigan philosopher Elizabeth Anderson explains the effects of social position on inquiry. Neither principle means an accusation must be believed. They require a fair opportunity to test it. [S12–S13]

1. WHAT WAS THE ALLEGATION?
University of Turin historian Sergio Luzzatto reports that Elvira Serritelli told apostolic visitor Carlo Maccari in 1960 that she had been Pio’s mistress from 1922 to 1930 and that Cleonice Morcaldi succeeded her. His original-record locator is Positio IV, pp. 127–130. Ángel Peña’s supportive biography gives 1921–1930 instead. The one-year discrepancy remains unresolved. These are later publications reporting her alleged first-person testimony, not our inspection of her full signed statement. [S01, S10]

Two different claims require different evidence: (a) what Serritelli said happened to herself; (b) what she believed or knew about Morcaldi. Being a participant in the first would not automatically make her an eyewitness to the second. No second independent participant or eyewitness to intercourse has been recovered here.

The alleged encounters were in the 1920s. The testimony was taken decades later. Neither fact automatically decides credibility. The useful questions are whether there were earlier disclosures, a stable account, dated opportunities, specific details independently checkable, or demonstrable contradictions. We do not yet possess that comparison.

2. WHAT DOES “REPLACED” MEAN?
Capuchin author Br. Błażej Strzechmiński, an Antonianum graduate and seminary lecturer in Kraków, describes successive leadership of the women’s devotional circle: Nina Campanile, Elvira in 1921–1930, and Cleonice from 1930 until Pio’s death. This establishes a published institutional account of leadership succession. Its further explanation—Elvira retaliated from jealousy and her accusation was false—is an interpretation requiring its own evidence. [S08]

The 1930 date consequently appears in both the succession narrative and the sexual allegation. That overlap provides a concrete change of relationship to investigate; it proves neither a sexual affair nor an invented accusation. Loss of status can motivate an untrue allegation. It can also be the occasion on which a true account is disclosed. Motive alone cannot choose between those possibilities.

3. RESTORING MORCALDI’S OWN VOICE
The sanctuary’s biography says Morcaldi first met Pio in 1920, when she was about sixteen, and later renewed vows of poverty, chastity and obedience in his hands. It also records her writing, charity fundraising and preservation of testimony. That biography is favorable institutional evidence, not a deposition. [S07]

Her own published Easter 1962 diary excerpt interprets Pio through devotion to Jesus, sacrifice and parental care. It protests his restrictions. This is evidence of her own expressed religious understanding and commitment. The publication is selected and later; the autograph and complete editorial history remain unchecked. [S06]

Capuchin historian/theologian Luciano Lotti, in a paper hosted by the Diocese of Ancona-Osimo, quotes Morcaldi’s published diary, 1997 edition p. 87. She understood Pio as supplying maternal care after her mother’s death; “Mammina” belongs to that context. An affectionate parental or mystical vocabulary cannot simply be translated into an admission of physical sex. [S09]

Equally, her religious interpretation does not settle every question about boundaries. Spiritual authority, confession, parental language, obedience and preferred access can create an unequal relationship. The question is whether that authority was exploited, and what actions and choices the records actually establish. Her adolescent first meeting warrants careful chronology; it does not by itself prove grooming, a sexual relationship or abuse. The alleged 1930 succession occurred when she was about twenty-six.

A feminist reading should not erase her agency by presuming she was a helpless victim. Nor should it demand that someone name an experience “abuse” before unequal power can be examined. Both cautions matter here.

4. THE DEFENSES THAT NEED AUDITING
Some favorable accounts call Serritelli sexually obsessed or mentally ill. Strzechmiński refers to doctors without supplying their names or clinical records. Peña describes alleged earlier false accusations without giving us the original complaints and findings. These statements are research leads, not established diagnoses or demonstrated falsity. A psychiatric condition, even if documented, would require a specific connection to reliability on this account. Trauma would not establish a propensity to invent an affair. [S08, S10]

Postulator Gerardo Di Flumeri’s reported 1999 reply invoked the women’s age and appearance. That is an unsound way to exclude sexual activity. Their age in 1960 also cannot resolve an allegation about the 1920s. Discrediting this defense does not establish the accusation. It means a better defense must rest on actual evidence. [S03]

Vatican journalist Marco Tosatti reproduces witnesses hostile to Serritelli’s credibility, including Antonio Bianchi and a chain involving Giosuè Fini, Maria Massa and Elvira’s sister, named “Manetta” in that text. Their interpretations and repeated psychiatric labels do not constitute multiple eyewitnesses to the alleged sexual encounters. The chain must be kept visible. A reported hostile gesture by a sister could support resentment; it cannot alone prove the underlying allegation false. The same book’s humiliating-penance story is multiple hearsay and is not treated here as an established act of abuse. [S11]

Critics’ accounts also contain gendered judgments about women’s bodies, emotions and supposed instability. Those descriptions deserve the same scrutiny as the defenders’ labels. A feminist investigation cannot selectively police only the side it dislikes.

5. WHAT CORROBORATION OR COUNTEREVIDENCE DO WE ACTUALLY HAVE?
Published accounts of the 1960 surveillance describe disputed sounds interpreted as a kiss and suspicions about private contact. The recordings have not been authenticated and independently audited in this research. A kiss, even if established, would need context and would not prove the earlier affair. Disputed claims about recordings of confession also require separate treatment; procedural misconduct does not make the affair true or false. [S02, S10–S11]

Peña quotes Fr. Pellegrino, citing Positio II p. 241, as having secretly watched three meetings on different days and found proper conduct. This is concrete counter-observation, stronger than merely calling women unattractive. Its coverage remains three observed meetings, not the entire previous forty years. The original full deposition remains unread. [S10]

The official favorable judgment matters: the Vatican’s 1997 decree affirmed heroic virtues, including chastity. Postulator Paolino Rossi’s 1999 process account states that Maccari and Bishop Girolamo Bortignon were heard ex officio. These facts contradict a suggestion that adverse voices were simply absent. They do not disclose every question, contradiction or reason for rejecting this particular allegation. Rossi also reports a proposed witness barred by superiors; that witness’s identity and reason are not recovered, so no inference that it was a suppressed accuser is justified. [S04–S05]

6. CORRECTION TO OUR EARLIER ACCOUNT
I earlier stated too categorically that Maccari himself considered Serritelli unreliable. Luzzatto writes that Maccari suspended judgment and suggested disbelief in her self-accusation while remaining concerned about Morcaldi. That is the historian’s reading of the report. We have not yet verified an explicit, reasoned rejection in Maccari’s complete text. An investigator doubting Serritelli is also different from conclusively clearing every allegation involving another woman. [S01]

This qualification has been added to the earlier local notes. It is not a finding that Pio was guilty, and no new debate point has been awarded. The closed chemical challenge is not reopened by re-listing its historical advocates.

7. DISSENTERS: HOW THE REGISTER IS ORGANIZED
The accompanying register records 34 named entries: critical, mixed, administrative or unresolved voices (including two unresolved name mentions). It is not “34 independent accusations.” Three are specifically verified as later cause witnesses in the recovered accounts: Maccari, Bortignon and Giustino. Others supplied older documents, discussed administration, participated in surveillance, or remain secondary-source leads. Changes of position and favorable observations are retained. Closely connected reports are not multiplied into independent evidence.

Pio, Morcaldi, Raffaello Carlo Rossi, Di Flumeri, Paolino Rossi and supportive credibility witnesses are described separately in the source trail; they are not mislabeled dissenters. The list covers the names recovered in our available corpus. Without the complete witness roll, Positio and consultors’ votes, claiming it is every dissenter who ever existed would be unjustified.

8. THE NEXT DOCUMENTS THAT WOULD ACTUALLY ADVANCE THIS CASE
A. Complete Serritelli deposition and any original recording/transcript, including questions, dates, oath and signatures: start with Positio IV pp. 127–130, then identify the underlying original. Determine whether this section contains her verbatim testimony or Maccari’s summary.
B. Maccari’s full report, credibility reasoning and corroboration, plus diary and later memorandum: Positio IV pp. 103–141, 160–171, 419–442. References are supplied as archival leads, not claims that every page has been read.
C. The cause’s actual response on this allegation and relevant consultors’ reasoning, including what specific facts defeated it. The general virtues decree and public postulator replies cannot substitute for this.
D. Morcaldi’s dated full diary and letters, especially any original secrecy/access requests. An isolated note reproduced in journalism is a finding aid, not proof of sex. Compare dates with restrictions and full exchanges; distinguish who is speaking.
E. Original medical/psychological assessments invoked to dismiss Serritelli, and the records behind alleged prior false complaints. If no such records can be identified, the labels should carry little weight.
F. Pellegrino’s complete deposition, and separate statements by Giustino, Terenzi, Bortignon and the credibility witnesses. Test independence and exact personal knowledge.

CURRENT ASSESSMENT
We have a substantial allegation and reasons to audit how it was handled. We do not have a demonstrated affair or a demonstrated fabricated complaint. The strongest feminist criticism presently established concerns credibility reasoning and unequal access to the women’s own records. It challenges the adequacy of some published defenses. It does not, on its own, overturn the chastity finding or the separate stigmata evidence.

The useful standard is symmetrical: test Serritelli’s account, Morcaldi’s own account, the critics’ inferences and the defenders’ rebuttals against specific documents. Neither sainthood nor an accusation should make that examination unnecessary.

The Shroud argument and standards for concession
A cumulative case and a proposed shared test
Updated 28 October 2026
The proposed rule is to prefer the explanation that best accounts for the combined evidence under explicitly stated assumptions, and to concede that preference without requiring certainty. Christian supernatural explanations remain eligible. The inquiry now develops the independent modern miracle case before revisiting earlier miracles and the Shroud. Daniel judges the Fátima evidence sufficient for a preponderance finding; the assistant has conceded substantial positive evidence while remaining uncertain about the cause.
Your cumulative argument
Your argument treats the Shroud as a possible historical anchor for a Christian worldview with substantial philosophical and historical defenses. Its force depends on the connection between those strands. The numbered chain records your position; the later sections distinguish that position from established findings and unresolved premises.
1. Begin with a defended worldview. Christianity offers an account of a creator, incarnation, redemption and Resurrection. You appeal to its long philosophical and theological development, including Aquinas, as reasons to treat this account as a serious candidate before examining the cloth. The worldview is not being invented to explain one unusual object. [1]
2. Add the historical case. You connect the philosophical account with the Gospel testimony, Paul and Luke, the first disciples' conviction and courage, and the Temple prophecy argument. These are separate proposed lines of support, whose dating, authorship and historical premises still need individual assessment. Their strength should not be assumed merely by listing them.
3. Locate the burial cloth within that history. John 20 describes burial cloths and a disciple who saw and believed. You invoke this as scriptural context for Jesus' burial cloth, rather than insisting that the text explicitly describes the Turin image or identifies that surviving object. The connection to Turin remains a further historical claim. [2]
3a. Add the earlier textual record. Twelve pre-1350 texts in the accompanying inventory attest traditions of preserved burial cloths, miraculous images on cloth, or a displayed shroud bearing Christ’s figure. The tenth–eleventh-century Latin Tractatus explicitly describes a whole-body impression on linen; Gervase repeats that tradition before 1215; Robert de Clari reports a shroud displaying Christ’s figure in Constantinople before the 1204 sack. These are earlier descriptions relevant to the kind of object later securely documented at Lirey. Their value is as a cumulative historical supporting point. The texts differ in specificity, some depend on earlier traditions, and identifying their objects with Turin remains disputed. [17–20]
4. Explain why the anchor matters. You identify Christianity as a major source of your own culture's moral inheritance and a culturally dominant historical tradition. An authentic relic would therefore connect an existing intellectual and lived tradition to material history. Cultural influence explains the significance of that connection; its magnitude alone does not establish authenticity.

Modern miracles as independent background evidence
The latest argument begins with Daniel’s conviction that modern Christian miracles already establish a relevant history of divine action. In his view, the Shroud’s supernatural explanation is therefore supported by an independently defended Christian framework. Some of what the conversation called differing priors is better described as disagreement over the strength of other proposed evidence. The modern cases need assessment in their own right, and any supported conclusion must carry forward into the Shroud comparison.
The proposed sequence
First assess Fátima, Zeitoun and Padre Pio using their contemporary records, witnesses, images and scientific claims. Next consider what any established modern precedent contributes to earlier miracle reports, including Joseph of Cupertino and Lourdes. Finally revisit the Shroud with that revised background assessment, alongside its own dating, history, image and forensic evidence. Lourdes began after the invention of photography; Joseph is a pre-photography case. The contrast concerns the available evidentiary setting rather than a single chronological dividing line.
This sequence is conditional rather than automatic. Establishing one supernatural event would undermine a categorical exclusion of supernatural causes. Establishing specifically Christian divine action would add relevant support to other Christian claims. It would not identify the Turin cloth or settle every earlier report without further evidence. The same Shroud observations must not be counted once to establish the modern background and again as independent object evidence.
What vindicatory means in this argument
Daniel uses vindicatory miracle for a public sign explicitly offered as evidence for the truth of an apparition or religious message. His argument concerns a promised sign followed by a public event, rather than religious interpretation of unexplained strangeness alone. His theology further holds that this specifically Catholic form of authentication is unique to the Catholic Church. That uniqueness is a separate comparative claim; a survey of other traditions has not been completed.
Zeitoun contributes roughly 90 reported appearances over three years (1968–1971), with peak crowds estimated at 250,000 or more and likely over one million unique viewers across the period, according to Fr Anthony Mourad. These figures describe the reported scale; the unique-viewer total is an estimate, not a verified count. Daniel regards the silent appearances at the Coptic Orthodox church as major public Christian supernatural evidence, while distinguishing them from an explicit spoken authentication of a distinctively Catholic message. The cumulative Fátima argument
Daniel’s case combines an advance promise, a large public gathering, contemporary reporting, accounts from separated places and proposed physical corroboration. Its force is the relationship among those strands. He maintains that the prediction and public testimony stand independently of the photographic reconstruction, with the photographs supplying additional evidence. [26]
The promise and its stated evidentiary purpose
The parish priest’s record dated 14 July 1917 reports Lúcia asking for a miracle so everyone would believe and receiving a promise concerning three months later. Manuel Nunes Formigão’s interrogation dated 27 September explicitly connects the coming 13 October with making the people believe that the Lady really appears. The intended authentication is therefore present in pre-event records. [25]
The assistant conceded that the promise’s evidentiary purpose and its date and place deserve substantive weight. Daniel’s argument does not require advance specification of every later color or movement. A remaining comparative question is how strongly the subsequent experience fulfilled the promise against the range of events that an expectant gathering might have interpreted as fulfillment. Calling the promise broad cannot replace that actual comparison.
The public occurrence and the common event
The argument concerns a gathering of tens of thousands, contemporary reports including secular or anticlerical newspapers, and witnesses describing extraordinary light and apparent solar behavior. The crowd estimate is not a count of independently preserved testimony, and an outlet’s political position does not establish each reporter’s personal atheism. Those distinctions preserve the strength of the contemporary public record without inflating it. [25] [26]
Daniel identifies the common event as a luminous phenomenon in the apparent solar region that witnesses could watch and that behaved extraordinarily. Calling it the Sun reports their language; the proposed supernatural explanation does not require the astronomical Sun to leave its orbit. Identifying the source as a separate local emitter remains an inference, rather than a fact established merely by the terminology.
The participants need not all report an identical experience. Some describe rotation, others apparent descent, changing colors or landscape illumination; some describe religious figures, including the Holy Family. The assistant conceded that variation is not automatically a contradiction and that a missing detail is not a denial. The relevant test is whether accounts are compatible under differences of phase, viewpoint and attention, and whether explicit disagreements affect the central inference.

Physical corroboration and the photographic debate
The historical case stands independently of the photographs
The assistant conceded that a failure of Dalleur’s reconstruction would remove a supporting argument without disposing of the advance promise, public reporting or distant testimony. Historical testimony can in principle support a miracle conclusion. Requiring a photograph after agreeing to a comparative historical standard would introduce an additional requirement that Daniel’s argument never depended on.
The light source elevation
Philippe Dalleur of the Pontifical University of the Holy Cross infers an elevation near 25–32 degrees for a directional source in photograph D115, against a solar elevation near 42.7 degrees. The discrepancy is about 11–18 degrees, with a central estimate near 14.5 degrees. Earlier conversational figures such as a 15–30 degree discrepancy should not be carried forward as the calculated result. A still photograph does not itself record the source diving. [27]
Dalleur’s construction uses corresponding object and shadow features and projective geometry. Sloping ground alone does not invalidate that geometry. His website includes real photographs as well as synthetic examples with known sources. Worked control examples support the method’s plausibility; they do not by themselves establish an error bound for ambiguous D115 features or all combined uncertainties.
Mark Grant’s simulated bright cloud illustrates a potential feature selection failure. Dalleur’s reply, reproduced in correspondence by Muse, argues that it does not reproduce the sharper hat, finger, stick and stone constraints in D115. Camera calibration, shadow identification and combined uncertainty remain substantive disagreements. No independent completed reconstruction resolving that dispute was located. The supporters identified include Muse, Princeton philosophy doctoral students Joe Schmid and Noah McKay, and favorable discussion by Harvard Human Flourishing Program researchers Tim Lomas and Brendan Case. Their discussion should not be counted as independent optical replication. [26] [27] [35]
Rain and reports of rapid drying
Preceding rain and widespread wet conditions were accepted as well documented. The remaining question is the reported extent and speed of drying, not whether it had rained. A photograph of a boy’s clothing supplies a proposed wet and dry contrast linked to his jacket. Identifying that pattern as selective infrared drying is an inference from an ordinary photograph, not a direct infrared measurement. Uncertainty about that boy’s exact clothing sequence does not erase the wider drying testimony. [26] [27]
Comfortable viewing and retinal injury
Many witnesses describe watching a pale disc comfortably. Daniel argues that this is difficult to reconcile with prolonged direct exposure to the normal bright midday Sun and supports a distinct luminous phenomenon. The assistant accepted the observation as relevant positive evidence, while preserving a medical distinction: comfortable viewing and no identified cluster of subsequent injury reports are not a clinical examination establishing no retinal injury among all attendees. Retinal injury can be painless and symptoms delayed. No systematic examinations of the crowd were found in the reviewed sources. [26] [33]
The disagreements and concessions after Fátima
The natural explanation still under consideration
The strongest natural contender discussed combines cloud filtered sunlight and visual adaptation with expectation, differences of attention and later recollection. This is a plausible incomplete hypothesis, rather than an established reconstruction of the complete event. The assistant corrected an overbroad impression that no plausible natural account existed. A cause need not explain every observation through one mechanism, but each added mechanism needs evidential support.
Pinto Coelho, a Catholic lawyer present at Fátima, described colors, apparent rotation, approach and heat, then reported similar colors and rotation during a later solar observation. This supplies contemporary affirmative support for natural explanations of some features. It does not establish repetition of the entire predicted event. Muse replies that Brochado questioned Coelho’s comparison and that Maria de Jesus Raposo distinguished later solar effects from the Cova occurrence. Political intention alone cannot settle whose comparison was more accurate. [32]
What the assistant conceded
The cumulative case is substantive positive evidence. The public promise’s evidentiary purpose is explicit before the event. The historical case does not depend on photographs. Differing visual experiences do not automatically contradict a common occurrence. Distant reports must receive their own explanation. The assistant has not demonstrated a natural reconstruction convincingly covering the complete record or shown natural alternatives to outweigh the combined case. Incomplete review is a limit on the assistant’s assessment, not evidence against Daniel.
What remains a disagreement
Daniel judges the combined evidence to favor a Christian supernatural fulfillment by a preponderance. The assistant remains uncertain whether that threshold is crossed. It has not conceded supernatural causation as an established finding. The unresolved comparison concerns how reliably the evidence establishes an external physical anomaly and how the complete pattern weighs against the collective natural alternatives. Failure of one rebuttal is not automatically a proof of the competing explanation; mere natural possibility is not automatically a reason to prefer it.

How evidence must accumulate across cases
One sufficiently strong event could warrant a supernatural conclusion. Several independently supported events can jointly do so even when each is individually inconclusive. There is no fixed required number. A rule that names a possible natural cause for each event and then resets its evidential contribution to zero prevents cumulative reasoning from working. The assistant explicitly rejected that rule.
The relevant threshold is a comparative judgment that supernatural action is better supported overall, rather than exhaustion of every imaginable natural possibility. The same standards apply to both explanations. Shared sources, common expectations, selection effects and causal dependence affect how much evidence can be counted independently. A numerical product is inappropriate without defensible probabilities and dependence assumptions.
The contribution of the cases can differ. A securely recorded prediction linked to a message may support purposeful agency more directly than an unexplained occurrence alone. Zeitoun could add independent evidence for public Christian supernatural activity even if it lacks the same explicit predictive and spoken authentication. A supported conclusion must be carried into the older miracle inquiry and the Shroud rather than silently discarded at each transition.

No comparably documented counterpart to the complete Christian combination was established. The added comparison section records the strongest candidates and their actual witness and duration figures. The artifact and the cumulative conclusion
5. Take the physical object seriously. You emphasize the combination of superficial coloration, the reported absence of applied pigment as the body-image medium, negative-like tonal properties and anatomically suggestive form. Your case concerns that combination, rather than one surprising feature considered alone. The precise scope and evidential status of these claims are recorded in the evidence ledger. [3,4]
6. Ask for a complete artificial account. A manufacturing proposal should account for the relevant chemistry, image structure and geometry together, within a historically defensible setting. A partial reproduction or the statement that a clever artist might have existed does not by itself supply that account. You argue that a medieval origin would be exceptionally anomalous in art history. [4,6] No securely dated medieval comparator matching the combination of reported microscopic superficiality and negative-like bodily appearance has been identified in the sources reviewed. This historical comparison should be kept distinct from modern experiments, whose fidelity remains debated. [21,22]
7. Treat the negative-like image as additional evidence. You described photographic negativity as the cherry on top. On a medieval dating, that negative-like behavior would precede photographic technology by centuries. An accidental negative-like result could be causally possible, but its contribution must be explained alongside the image's other properties. A modern photograph of a sculpture, or a generic 3D rendering, does not demonstrate how this particular linen image was made.
8. Interpret anatomical distortions through the cloth. You argue that apparent distortions should be tested against a cloth draped over a three-dimensional body. Published studies report anatomical compatibility under fitted posture and wrapping assumptions. You have pressed for a fair comparison with medieval techniques and surviving counterparts. Claims of independently recovered perfect anatomy go beyond those fitting results. [4,5]
9. Challenge the representativeness of the dated material. The three laboratories measured portions from one sampling location. You argue that a repaired or otherwise unrepresentative corner could therefore generate mutually consistent technical results without dating the entire cloth correctly. You cite replacement arguments and alternative aging studies as reasons to reopen the question. The documented handling, fire damage and repairs make representativeness a substantive issue. You regard a shared sampling weakness as fatal to treating the result as conclusive for the original cloth; that does not require denying the laboratories' isotope measurements. [7-14]
10. Compare the explanatory commitments. You argue that an artificial account accumulates unsupported exceptions, while the relic account places the object within a worldview already defended on other grounds. Its relevant simplicity is explanatory unity. A case that requires several independent special assumptions should bear their cost; a single causal process that explains several features should receive appropriate credit.
11. Let independent strands reinforce one another. If the cloth is Jesus' authentic burial relic, you argue that it provides a material anchor for the Christian historical account. If its image is associated with the Resurrection, the connection becomes stronger still. Independent philosophical and historical reasons can make that identification more credible in return. Their support must be counted once, with dependencies identified.
12. Draw a comparative conclusion. Your conclusion is that the relic account is substantially more likely than an artificial account, rather than merely possible. You previously proposed 98 to 2 odds. That number expresses your confidence; this document does not treat it as a measured or calculated probability.
Earlier descriptions of burial cloths and images
The historical supporting point is that relevant descriptions preceded the Shroud’s securely identifiable appearance at Lirey in the 1350s. The whole-body image tradition is documented centuries earlier. Robert de Clari’s reported shroud display precedes Lirey by roughly 150 years. A medieval-origin account must be considered in the context of those existing traditions; they also provide possible antecedents for the relic account. Prior traditions could inform either the identification of an old relic or the production and reception of a later object. [17–20]
The inventory contains twelve separate texts, not twelve independent eyewitnesses or twelve secure identifications of the same surviving cloth. It is a defined selection of relevant witnesses, not an exhaustive count of every medieval mention. Their documentary existence establishes the age and content of the traditions; their legends and relic identifications require separate assessment. Dates below refer to the texts or surviving textual witnesses, rather than automatically to the events they narrate.
The central formulation
Christian accounts of preserved burial linens and images impressed on cloth long predate Lirey. More specifically, a whole-body image on linen is described in a tenth–eleventh-century manuscript tradition, and a shroud displaying Christ’s figure is reported in Constantinople before 1204. The surviving Turin cloth therefore has possible antecedents in earlier Christian texts. Together with its distinctive physical characteristics, this earlier record forms a supporting strand in the cumulative relic argument; the disputed connection between the earlier objects and Turin sets the strength of that strand.
How to interpret incomplete descriptions
A large textile, limited opportunities for examination, difficult travel, language differences and devotional rather than technical writing provide plausible reasons for incomplete descriptions. Photography in 1898 made its negative-like appearance and some features much easier to recognize. These considerations reduce the expectation of a modern technical description in earlier sources. They cannot by themselves turn a face-only account into a description of a concealed full-length burial cloth. Earlier observers could see the image, as the detailed 1534 repair account illustrates. [23,24]
Twelve textual witnesses before 1350
1. Jerome  c. 392–393
De viris illustribus 2. Quotes the Gospel according to the Hebrews: the risen Jesus gives his burial cloth to the servant of the priest. Early tradition of preservation or transfer. No image, surviving location or Turin identification.
Source: De viris illustribus 2
2. Evagrius Scholasticus  c. 593–594
Ecclesiastical History 4.27. Describes a divinely made image sent by Christ to Abgar, used in the defense of Edessa during the Persian siege of 544. Firm evidence that this image tradition existed in the sixth century; the reported event and identification with Turin require further argument.
Source: Ecclesiastical History 4.27
3. Anonymous  Between 609 and 944
Acts of Thaddaeus. Jesus washes and dries his face, leaving its image on a cloth described with the Greek term tetradiplon. A genuine pre-Lirey cloth-image text. Often dated to the seventh century; the wider dating range is retained. The hidden full-length Shroud interpretation is a modern hypothesis.
Source: Acts of Thaddaeus
4. John of Damascus  Eighth century
On the Orthodox Faith 4.16. Recounts Christ impressing his face on a garment and sending it to Abgar when a painter could not portray him. Explicit witness from a Doctor of the Church to the cloth-image tradition. Describes a living Christ and face impression.
Source: On the Orthodox Faith 4.16
5. Stephen III, as quoted by Hadrian I  769; quoted in 793
Hadrian I, letter on images to Charlemagne, section 18. Hadrian quotes Stephen’s Roman council testimony: Christ sent Abgar the likeness of his face transferred onto linen. Western papal reception of the tradition. The transmitted wording is face-only: the later whole-body wording must not be backdated to 769.
Source: Hadrian I, letter on images to Charlemagne, section 18. Nicolotti 2014, pp. 114–115, especially note 74; primary edition Epistolae Karolini aevi III (1899), p. 23.
6. Anonymous imperial court account  c. 944–945
Narratio de imagine Edessena. Recounts the Edessa image’s origins and its translation to Constantinople in 944, including alternative accounts of the image’s formation. Documents the imperial reception and associated legends. Transfer of the Edessa image is historical; its identity with Turin is disputed.
Source: Narratio de imagine Edessena
7. Gregory Referendarius  944
Sermon for the reception of the Edessa image. Discusses an image formed through Christ’s sweat and draws connections with the blood and water of his Passion. Contemporary reception text. Whether the side-wound language describes blood on the object or a theological comparison is disputed; it is not an uncontested full-body description.
Source: Sermon for the reception of the Edessa image
8. Anonymous Latin author  Tenth–eleventh-century manuscript
Tractatus ex libro Syrorum; Codex Vossianus Latinus Q69, fols. 6r–6v. Describes Christ lying on white linen and transferring the likeness of his whole body onto it, in the Abgar story. An explicit whole-body image-on-linen tradition centuries before Lirey. Its living-Christ narrative also includes an image changing age at Easter; it cannot be treated as a straightforward physical inspection.
Source: Tractatus ex libro Syrorum; Codex Vossianus Latinus Q69, fols. 6r–6v. Nicolotti 2014, pp. 112–116, with the Latin text and manuscript discussion.
9. Orderic Vitalis  Early twelfth century; before 1142
Ecclesiastical History, Book 9, Edessa passage. Describes the Abgar cloth as used to wipe Christ’s face, then refers to the lineaments and proportions of his divine body. Relevant bodily language, but the immediate context remains a face-wiping story. This is weaker than an unequivocal full-length description; no direct inspection is established.
Source: Ecclesiastical History, Book 9, Edessa passage. Primary passage in Marjorie Chibnall’s edition and translation, Ecclesiastical History, vol. V, Book IX. Quotation located through Markwardt 2014; edition not independently collated in this update.
10. Nicholas Mesarites  c. 1200–1201
Account of the revolt of John Komnenos, section 13. Lists linen burial cloths of Christ among the relics in the Pharos chapel at Constantinople. A burial-linen relic claim in the right broad historical setting. Uses the plural and does not describe a body image; descriptions of a visible outline added in some modern retellings are unsupported.
Source: Account of the revolt of John Komnenos, section 13. Nicolotti 2014, pp. 106–109, including the Greek text and translation.
11. Robert de Clari  Written after 1204, usually c. 1216
The Conquest of Constantinople, chapter 92. Reports a shroud at Blachernae before the sack of 1204, displayed upright on Fridays so that Christ’s figure could be seen; says its subsequent whereabouts were unknown. One of the most pertinent possible antecedents: a burial shroud with a visible figure. Does not give dimensions, an explicit front-and-back description, or a route to Lirey.
Source: The Conquest of Constantinople, chapter 92. Primary Old French quotation and translation in Nicolotti 2011, p. 154 and note 19.
12. Gervase of Tilbury  c. 1212–1215
Otia imperialia 3.24. Recounts the whole-body impression on linen in the Abgar story, together with its display and changes of age at Easter. Explicit whole-body image tradition before Lirey, closely dependent on the Latin tradition represented by entry 8. A separate text, not a second independent observation.
Source: Otia imperialia 3.24. Nicolotti 2014, p. 114, note 72; Banks and Binns edition and translation (2002), p. 596.

Handling repairs and the sampling argument
The preservation findings strengthen the case for testing whether the dated corner represents the original cloth. A textile can remain well preserved overall while containing local repairs and altered regions. Three laboratories measuring one region cannot independently establish that region's relationship to the rest of the object.
The documented history
The custodians record serious fire damage in 1532, followed in 1534 by linen patches and a supporting Holland-cloth lining. Their restoration account describes removing the old patches and lining, clearing residues and attaching a new support in 2002. These are documented interventions, not hypothetical damage inferred from the cloth's age. [13,14]
Textile conservator Mechthild Flury-Lemberg, who established the Abegg Foundation's textile department and led the 2002 intervention, describes healthy surviving linen alongside severely deteriorated areas. Her report explains why damaged regions made support necessary for vertical display. Good surviving fabric therefore does not establish uniform preservation or the absence of later additions. [12]
Fr Andrew Dalton describes historical bare-hand display as a possible source of corner wear at approximately 0:35–0:40 in the interview. This provides a proposed reason for repair. The secure date in the conservation record is the addition of the lining in 1534; it does not establish that every earlier display was unsupported or that direct handling ended then. Nor does a depiction of handling establish a repair in the precise 1988 strip. [15]
What the 1988 design could establish
Paul Damon of the University of Arizona Department of Geosciences and colleagues explicitly acknowledged potential contamination. Their paper reports different cleaning procedures and selection away from visible patches and charred areas. All three laboratories nevertheless received portions of a single approximately 10 by 70 mm strip. Controls and cleaning address laboratory performance and some contamination; they do not provide a second Shroud sampling location. [7]
The conclusion to carry into the cumulative case
A shared sampling defect would survive agreement among laboratories. If the dated strip contains substantial later replacement material, technically accurate measurements of that material would not date the original cloth. The history therefore supports treating representativeness as an unresolved premise when using the medieval result to exclude a first-century cloth. It does not establish the repair or a sufficient surviving contaminant in that strip. [7,8,11]
This objection targets the reach of the conclusion. It does not make the 1988 results worthless. Flury-Lemberg disputes invisible reweaving, and retained-fragment microscopy provides counterevidence to some repair accounts. New, independently characterized samples from separated original-weave locations would test the common sampling concern directly. [11,16]

Define the question before comparing explanations
The factual binary
Let R mean that this surviving cloth was used in Jesus' burial. R is true or false. Its negation includes every history in which it was not Jesus' burial cloth. Uncertainty about which history occurred is a state of our knowledge, not an additional physical origin.
Medieval artwork is one possible way for R to be false. It is not the entire logical complement. An older cloth associated with another person is a different category. An ancient textile bearing a later manufactured image is another possible category. These examples identify logical alternatives; naming them provides no evidence that any one actually occurred.
Let M mean that the body image was produced through the Resurrection. Even if R were established, M would require a further inference about image formation. An authentic burial cloth with an ordinary image-forming process would make R true while leaving M false. We should specify whether a concession concerns R, M, a manufacturing proposal, or Christianity more broadly.
The proposed method
Use comparative historical inference with Bayesian bookkeeping. Bayesian reasoning makes us ask how the evidence changes the relative credibility of the hypotheses, given our background reasons. It does not require inventing precise probabilities for poorly understood processes.
Keep two bodies of support distinct. Background support includes independently assessed philosophical and Christian historical arguments. Object evidence includes dating, chemistry, image geometry and provenance. Starting with Christian background support is legitimate. Reusing Shroud evidence in that starting assessment and then counting it again as new evidence would inflate the result.
Compare the relic claim with the alternatives collectively. Beating each named alternative individually does not necessarily make R more likely than all alternatives combined. An unspecified residual possibility should receive a justified uncertainty allowance, rather than an unlimited supply of explanatory credit.
Apply the same explanatory standard
An artificial account must explain why the observed features arise through its proposed processes. A religious account must explain why these particular observations are supported by the specified religious history or event. The bare capacities of an inventive human or an omnipotent creator are insufficient to establish which history occurred.
Count independent assumptions and causal connections, rather than rhetorical length. Several image properties may result from one mechanism and should not be multiplied as independent improbabilities. A single sentence identifying a relic may contain several substantive commitments. Explanatory unity matters, but the number of sentences does not measure it.

When concession is warranted
Agree to the standard before declaring a winner
The working threshold for a comparative concession is that one specified proposition is better supported than its complement after considering the combined evidence and reasonable uncertainty. The later conversation uses a preponderance or more likely than not standard, without claiming a defensible numerical posterior. Daniel judges Fátima to clear that threshold; the assistant remains uncertain. Absolute certainty and elimination of every conceivable natural possibility are not required.
We should state the starting assumptions, identify the best evidence favoring each side, and say which unresolved issue controls the conclusion. Where probabilities cannot be defended, use explicit comparative judgments and test how they change under plausible assumptions. Counting supportive papers or adding informal scores is not a substitute.
What would warrant my concession
I should concede that R is the better explanation if the assessed dating, provenance, physical findings and independent Christian case jointly favor it over the combined alternatives, with the preference surviving reasonable challenges to the disputed premises. I must not withhold that conclusion simply because a miracle is involved, demand absolute proof after agreeing to a comparative standard, or treat an imagined technique as an established explanation.
I should concede M only if the evidence also favors the Resurrection as the image's cause over other histories compatible with R. That further conclusion can include philosophical and historical reasoning. It does not require a laboratory reproduction of a divine act, but it does require reasons that discriminate this particular explanation from the alternatives.
What would warrant your concession
You should concede against a first-century identity if representative, independently checked sampling and relevant controls reliably establish a later age for the image-bearing original textile, and the proposed explanations for that result fail their own tests. Authentic provenance establishing a later manufacture could also be decisive. The protocol and possible systematic errors must be assessed before relying on either result.
You should concede against a claim of impossible manufacture if a historically feasible process reproduces the agreed discriminating properties under independently examined conditions. Such a demonstration establishes feasibility; an attribution of actual manufacture would still need historical or material support. It would undermine an impossibility claim even without identifying the original maker.
A local concession can be required sooner
Either of us should revise a premise when its support fails. Examples include treating three laboratories as three independent sampling locations; calling a fitted model an independent perfect body scan; treating a repair as already proved; or describing a partial replica as a complete explanation. A local correction need not decide the entire case.
If neither explanation meets the agreed comparative standard, the justified result is a stated limit on our conclusion. Each participant should identify what would change it. Assigning either proposition a starting probability of exactly zero makes evidence-driven concession unavailable and should be disclosed.

The evidence we must weigh together
This ledger sets the claims that require assessment. It does not assign a verdict by majority vote or assume that every reported property is independently established.
The custom 3D exercise adds no independent authentication evidence. Its head depth came from a stock anatomical template, and the comparison showed meaningful mismatches. Those limitations describe our reconstruction and cannot decide the relic's origin.

Modern miracle comparisons across religions
The comparison strengthens the cumulative argument. The targeted search of modern Hindu, Jewish, Buddhist, Muslim and Sikh cases did not establish an evidential equivalent to the complete Fatima, Zeitoun and Padre Pio combination. It also did not establish a Jewish counterpart to Zeitoun's reported repeated mass visibility. Lists of other religions' miracles therefore do not, by themselves, answer this cumulative case. The search did find serious public reports and published bodily observations outside Christianity; their strengths and weaknesses must be compared individually.
Daniel's proposed explanation. The cases contribute different kinds of evidence. Fatima joins private Marian messages to an advance announcement and a public event. Zeitoun supplies reported repeated public Marian appearances across 1968-1971. Pio supplies bodily signs and fragrance reports across roughly fifty years, with identifiable medical examinations and close observers. Daniel argues that this diversity, persistence and religious coherence support intentional supernatural action within apostolic Christianity. A competing explanation must address the strongest supported features of each case, rather than dismiss all three because similar stories exist somewhere else.
The concession this search warrants. The assistant has not produced an equally documented modern counterpart to the combined case, or established an adequate ordinary explanation of these Christian events. A small-circle levitation story does not answer decades of public exposure; a large following is not a count of close observers of a miracle. Nor does a documented bodily anomaly in another tradition automatically explain away a Christian apparition. The same evidential standards apply in both directions.
The wording the evidence supports. The supported finding is that this targeted search did not establish a comparably documented match. The universal statement that zero other religions or worldviews have any comparable event has not been established by this bounded search. Pio's near-constant miraculous state is Daniel's interpretation of persistent signs and recurring reports; fragrance was not measured continuously for fifty years. Fatima and Pio are Roman Catholic cases, while Zeitoun is Coptic Orthodox. These settings fit his broader apostolic-Christian formulation, but their institutional setting alone does not establish exclusive doctrinal vindication or falsify Protestantism. The argument for divine agency and religious meaning remains explicit, rather than being silently converted into a measured fact.

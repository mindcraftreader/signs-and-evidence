**The strongest other route is to identify a manufacturing fingerprint—a feature that points to a particular human process—and reproduce it.** That could establish manufacture without ever naming the maker.

I’m happy to develop the medieval hypothesis as our working alternative. Here are the routes that could genuinely move it forward:

**1. Find a distinctive model or template.**  
A surviving sculpture, relief or drawing could share specific irregularities with the Shroud: an unusual asymmetry, misplaced feature or repeated geometric relationship. A match involving several peculiar details would be much stronger than a similar beard or pose. We would need comparisons against other artworks to check whether the resemblance is distinctive.

**2. Reproduce the important properties together.**  
University of Pavia chemist **Luigi Garlaschelli** published a physical reproduction; University of Padua engineer **Giulio Fanti** and **Thibault Heimburger** disputed its equivalence, particularly at the microscopic level. That disagreement supplies an experimental research program: agree beforehand on the discriminating properties, reproduce them using historically available materials, and have independent investigators examine the results. A successful process would substantially weaken the “unprecedented achievement” block. [Reproduction](https://doi.org/10.2352/J.ImagingSci.Technol.2010.54.4.040301), [published criticism](https://library.imaging.org/jist/articles/55/2/art00002)

**3. Find physical traces of fabrication on the original.**  
Possible targets include preparatory marks, a repeated transfer pattern, directional application traces or image-associated residues diagnostic of a particular process. These are proposed things to investigate—not features I’m claiming have been found. Their position and relationship to the original image would matter enormously: later devotional contact or conservation could also leave material.

**4. Establish a representative medieval textile date.**  
Independent medieval dates from several demonstrably original areas would remove much of the force of your replacement objection. That would establish a later cloth without identifying the image-making process. The Arizona, Oxford and ETH researchers’ original study sampled one region; the important improvement would be geographical coverage and textile characterization. [Original dating study](https://www.shroud.com/nature.htm)

**5. Find a documentary description of manufacture or acquisition.**  
A letter, inventory, delivery record or legal deposition could describe a newly made burial image with identifying details. It needn’t contain a payment. Conversely, a record describing acquisition of an already ancient cloth could strengthen your side. The crucial task is connecting the described object to Turin.

**6. Test the newer dating methods blindly.**  
Securely dated medieval linens could be submitted without disclosing their ages. If they repeatedly produced ancient estimates under the published procedures, that would explain part of the convergence you emphasize. If they performed reliably, your objection would strengthen. This tests a Jenga block; it would not independently prove manufacture.

**For work we can pursue now, I’d prioritize the template search, acquisition documents, and a precise comparison of published replicas with the original measurements.** A plausible commission establishes opportunity. A distinctive physical match or a reproducible manufacturing process could establish much more.

**Partly historical, partly modern. His successful aged replica was a controlled laboratory experiment—not a strict reconstruction of a fourteenth-century recipe.** My earlier wording should have made that clearer.

University of Pavia chemist **Luigi Garlaschelli** used:

- **A pigment carrying an acidic mixture**, rubbed onto linen over the model.
- **Controlled oven heating** to accelerate chemical discoloration.
- **Washing** to remove pigment, leaving a brownish image in the altered linen. [Experimental methods, pages 10–11](https://library.imaging.org/admin/apis/public/api/ist/website/downloadArticle/jist/54/4/art00002)

The important details are:

**The pigment was deliberately modern cobalt blue.** He chose blue so remaining pigment could be distinguished from brown discoloration of the linen. It served as an experimental marker; he wasn’t claiming medieval artists used that blue. Cambridge’s **Fitzwilliam Museum** dates this synthetic cobalt-aluminate pigment to the early nineteenth century. [Pigment history](https://unlocking-miniatures.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/terminology/)

**The effective chemical treatment used diluted sulfuric acid.** His final experiments used a roughly 1.1–1.3% preparation made from concentrated acid. He first tried several dry additives, but those did not leave the desired image after heating and washing.

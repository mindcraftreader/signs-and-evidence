**Mechanical testing is good at measuring a fiber’s strength. Using that strength to determine its age is much less established.**

University of Padua engineer **Giulio Fanti**, with **Pierandrea Malfi and University of Udine engineer Fabio Crosilla**, measured several properties—not just brittleness—including breaking strength, stiffness and behavior during repeated stretching. They calibrated these against reference textiles and obtained an ancient estimate for the Shroud. That is a serious experimental approach. But age, heat, moisture, processing and damage can affect the same properties. The difficult part is separating them. Their paper acknowledges these influences. [2015 study](https://doi.org/10.1051/matecconf/20153601001)

My assessment is **promising experimental dating, with insufficient independent validation to treat its stated uncertainty as universally reliable**. That gives its result some weight; it does not justify either dismissing it or treating it as an established replacement for carbon dating.

**Why weren’t the vacuumed fibers carbon-dated?** For the exact mechanical specimens, **I haven’t found a documented explanation from the authors**. There are practical obstacles:

- They tested only eight individual fibers, approximately millimeters long—different from eight whole threads.
- Their preparation mounted fibers using cyanoacrylate glue, introducing additional carbon. Untreated specimens would be preferable for a carbon comparison.
- Vacuum collections can contain original linen, backing fibers and other debris. Identifying what is being dated matters for either method. [Methods section](https://doi.org/10.1051/matecconf/20153601001)

**Smallness does not automatically make carbon dating impossible today.** ETH Zurich’s laboratory accepts research projects involving less than 100 micrograms of carbon, although sufficiently clean material is essential. [ETH laboratory](https://ams.ethz.ch/LIPServices/c14.html)

There is also a relevant development: **University of Padua geneticist Gianni Barcaccia and colleagues reported carbon dating two loose reliquary threads in 2026**. They obtained AD 1451–1622 and AD 1642–1800, interpreted as compatible with repairs. These were not the eight mechanical fibers or securely located original interior cloth. [2026 study](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-60684-7)

Your proposed comparison is therefore sensible: **mechanical testing and carbon dating on matched, securely identified material would be considerably more informative than comparing different collections.**

Routine AMS dating ideally uses about **1 milligram of purified carbon**, although specialist labs can measure much smaller amounts. Woods Hole reports routine measurements down to **20 micrograms**, with greater uncertainty; that is carbon remaining after cleaning, not the starting fiber weight. [Woods Hole laboratory](https://www2.whoi.edu/site/nosams/sample-requirements/standard-ams-analysis/)

We don’t have the mass of those eight fibers, so I shouldn’t assume that one fiber per lab would suffice. The email should ask about available untreated material and whether three independent measurements are feasible.

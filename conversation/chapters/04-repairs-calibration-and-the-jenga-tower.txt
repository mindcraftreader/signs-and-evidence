Your argument is: **a forgery explanation must explain the whole cloth—not just point to the medieval carbon date.** Here are the “Jenga blocks” you’ve asked it to carry.

1. **How was the picture made?**  
   An ordinary painting explanation does not explain all the reported features. Your question is: “How did someone put this picture on linen without leaving the usual signs of painting?”

2. **Why is the picture so thin?**  
   The body image is reported mainly on the outside of the fibres. A proposed method must explain how it coloured that tiny surface without soaking through like ordinary liquid.

3. **Why does it look like a real body?**  
   You point to the face, body shape and wounds. A forgery explanation must account for their realism and any stretching caused by cloth lying over a body. We have **not established perfectly measured anatomy**.

4. **Why does reversing its light and dark reveal a clearer face?**  
   That is your “cherry on top”: the negative-like effect existed long before photography. An accidental effect is possible, but merely calling it accidental does not explain how it happened.

5. **Why does the image contain information about shape?**  
   Some researchers connect image darkness with distance from a body. Your challenge is to explain that together with the chemistry. Turning brightness into bumps on a computer does not, by itself, prove a perfect hidden body scan.

6. **Where is the matching medieval technique?**  
   You ask for a historically believable method and comparable objects. “Some unknown genius did it” leaves work unfinished. Equally, not knowing the maker’s name does not by itself prove a miracle.

7. **Why do the newer dating methods point toward an older cloth?**  
   FTIR, Raman, mechanical testing and WAXS have produced ancient-compatible estimates. A medieval explanation must assess those results—not simply ignore them. These methods were developed principally by **Giulio Fanti’s University of Padua team** and **Liberato De Caro’s CNR Institute of Crystallography team**. [Spectroscopy study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.vibspec.2013.04.001), [WAXS study](https://doi.org/10.3390/heritage2040171).

8. **Why would several age estimates be wrong toward “older”?**  
   This is your strongest statistical question. But several clocks can share a problem—like several watches set using the same wrong clock. We still need enough independent checks to know their real error patterns before multiplying tiny probabilities.

9. **Can one corner speak for the whole cloth?**  
   All three carbon laboratories received material from one location. Repairs, added linings and handling make your concern concrete. Three good measurements cannot automatically fix a shared sampling mistake. The original paper confirms the single location. [Damon, University of Arizona Geosciences, and colleagues](https://www.shroud.com/nature.htm).

10. **Was that corner repaired?**  
    Documented needlework makes later additions a real historical possibility. The remaining question is whether substantial newer material entered **that particular strip**. The subagent is researching the historical reweaving technique now.

11. **Does its wider history fit the relic explanation?**  
    You also brought in the Passion accounts, proposed eastern pollen and biological traces, and Christianity’s existing philosophical case. Those belong in the comparison, although the biological traces do not currently establish a uniquely Palestinian, first-century origin.

**Where we stand:** your cumulative argument is recorded. The 75-paper screen is finished, but it did not uncover additional usable age comparisons that establish the claimed billion-to-one odds.

The key remaining disagreement is **how many of these blocks are separate surprises, and how many could come from one shared cause**. We cannot fairly assume either answer.

**How did carbon dating get established?**

Think of someone inventing a clock. Before trusting it, you give them objects with known ages and ask whether their clock gets the answers right.

That is what **James Arnold and Willard Libby at the University of Chicago** did. Their 1949 paper was explicitly titled *Age Determinations by Radiocarbon Content: Checks with Samples of Known Age*. They compared their measurements with independently dated material, including historically dated Egyptian objects. [Original paper](https://www.caeno.org/images/Libby_Science_1949.pdf)

The underlying clock is radioactive decay: after a plant dies, its carbon-14 decreases at a measurable rate. Ordinary heat and humidity do not speed up that nuclear decay as they can speed up damage to cloth. **Added carbon, exchanged carbon, and sampling the wrong material can still mislead the measurement.**

Importantly, the first version was imperfect. The amount of carbon-14 in the atmosphere had changed over time. Scientists checked measurements against independently dated tree rings and developed corrections. **Lloyd Currie of the National Institute of Standards and Technology** describes that history of testing, surprises, standards and improvements. [NIST study](https://www.nist.gov/publications/remarkable-metrological-history-radiocarbon-dating-ii)

Modern reliability therefore rests on several things together:

- Objects with independently known ages.
- Calibration against dated natural records.
- Reference materials and checks for laboratory contamination.
- Comparisons between independent laboratories, including their disagreements.

That last step is substantial: the fourth international radiocarbon intercomparison involved more than 90 laboratories. [FIRI study](https://doi.org/10.1002/jqs.702)

**Was the same basic approach used for the newer methods?**

**Yes, initially—but on a much smaller scale.**

| Method | What researchers established | What our review has not established |
|---|---|---|
| **FTIR and Raman** | Giulio Fanti of the University of Padua’s Industrial Engineering department and colleagues compared spectra from reference linens with their assigned ages and fitted age relationships. | A large, independent series showing how accurately those relationships predict previously unseen ancient linens. |
| **Mechanical dating** | Fanti and Pierandrea Malfi built equipment to measure individual fibres and related their properties to reference ages. | A broadly replicated error distribution across different burial, storage and treatment histories. |
| **WAXS** | Liberato De Caro of Italy’s CNR Institute of Crystallography and colleagues related structural deterioration to reference ages, with encouraging comparisons under specified conditions. | Extensive independent validation showing that the proposed clock remains accurate across unknown environmental histories. |

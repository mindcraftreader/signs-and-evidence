**Carbolic acid is the old name for phenol—*acido fenico* in Italian. It was a familiar disinfectant in medicine at the time.** The relevant Pio records begin in **1919**, earlier than the 1930s.

Its purpose was to **kill germs**. Glasgow surgery professor **Joseph Lister** famously used it on wound dressings, instruments and surgeons’ hands in the nineteenth century. Its presence among medical supplies had a straightforward explanation. [Science Museum’s history](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/people/cp37522/joseph-lister).

The complication is that **the same chemical can damage human tissue**. Concentration and exposure time matter: phenol can cause chemical burns and tissue death; even dilute solutions can burn with prolonged contact. [CDC/ATSDR medical guidance](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/MMG/MMGDetails.aspx?mmgid=144&toxid=27).

- **Was it connected to wound care?** Paolino’s later memoir says Pio used it to clean his wounds and stop bleeding. That is relevant evidence of reported application, although it does not establish that phenol created the wounds.

**All four use real scientific tools. The newer part is using their readings to work out the age of linen.** And yes: there are scholarly studies beyond the original Shroud results.

Think of linen as tiny ropes made from a plant. Over time, those ropes change. These four methods examine different kinds of change.

**1. FTIR: shine invisible light through the material**

Different parts of a molecule absorb different kinds of infrared light. A machine measures what gets absorbed. This produces a chemical “fingerprint.”

The dating idea is: **does that fingerprint change in a predictable way as linen gets older?**

FTIR instruments developed through several scientists’ work, including Albert Michelson’s optical equipment and Peter Fellgett’s work on measuring many wavelengths together. Computers helped make the instruments practical. [Scientific history](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1226565/)

For **dating linen**, Giulio Fanti, Pietro Baraldi, Roberto Basso and Anna Tinti published their method in **2013**. They compared linen whose ages were already known and built a chart connecting its light readings to its age. Eight suitable textiles were used for the infrared chart. [Original study](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/142241)

Other scholarly uses include:

- **Christina Margariti, 2019:** used FTIR to examine ancient burial textiles and understand their materials and preservation. This demonstrates its usefulness in archaeology. [Study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-019-0304-8)
- **César Barta and colleagues, 2025:** directly investigated the linen-dating approach, including how heating and neutron exposure affect it. This is a follow-up by a different author team. Its authors describe FTIR dating as less precise than radiocarbon, but potentially useful alongside it. [Study](https://e-archivo.uc3m.es/entities/publication/a611d541-1850-4383-b954-2acd0f7b6995)

**2. Raman: shine a laser and examine the light that comes back**

Imagine tapping a glass and listening to its sound. Raman spectroscopy does something roughly similar with light: it reveals how molecules vibrate.

The dating idea is: **do particular molecular vibrations change predictably as linen ages?**

The underlying light effect was discovered by **C. V. Raman and K. S. Krishnan in 1928**. It became an important tool for studying materials. [Discovery history](https://www.acs.org/education/whatischemistry/landmarks/ramaneffect.html)

The **linen-dating version** came from the same **Fanti–Baraldi–Basso–Tinti team in 2013**. They built their Raman chart using eleven suitable textiles of known age. [Original study](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/142241)

A useful follow-up is **Bonizzoni, Bruni, Fanti, Tiberio and Zaffino’s 2016 study**. They examined **23 historical fibers and 11 modern or treated fibers**. They found measurable changes connected with aging, but also with preservation conditions, heating and bleaching. That supports the scientific basis for examining aging this way. Fanti was a coauthor, so this was a wider collaboration rather than a fully independent check of his method. [Study](https://air.unimi.it/handle/2434/341803)

**3. Mechanical testing: gently pull a tiny fiber**

This measures:

- How much force it takes to break.
- How easily it stretches.
- How it behaves when repeatedly pulled and released.

The dating idea is: **can those measurements tell us how long the fiber has been aging?**

Pulling materials to test their strength is a longstanding engineering practice. **Giulio Fanti and Pierandrea Malfi developed this particular linen-dating method**, published online in **2013**, in a **2014 journal issue**. They used a specially designed machine and eleven selected textiles of known age to build their charts. Some tests break the individual fiber. [Original study](https://doi.org/10.1177/0040517513507366)

For other scholarly work, **Symington and colleagues’ 2009 study** tested flax and six other natural fibers for engineering uses. They examined strength, stretching, moisture and chemical treatment. This shows that the measurements themselves have established applications. It did **not** test Fanti and Malfi’s calendar-dating formula. [Study](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/7326/)

I have not found a large independent program that checks that dating formula on many previously unknown textiles.

**4. WAXS: use X-rays to examine the tiny structure inside the fiber**

Linen contains long chains of molecules. Those chains and their arrangement change as the material deteriorates.

WAXS measures the pattern made when X-rays scatter from that structure.

The dating idea is: **can we measure the structural changes and calculate how long they took?**

X-ray diffraction began with **Max von Laue and his collaborators’ work in 1912**. [History](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1914/laue/lecture/)

The **linen-dating formula** was published in **2019 by Liberato De Caro and six colleagues**, including Cinzia Giannini and Giulio Fanti. They combined measurements of known-age linen with a model of how cellulose ages. Temperature and moisture matter in that model. [Original study](https://doi.org/10.3390/heritage2040171)

There is meaningful supporting work:

- Their 2019 study checked **four additional ancient textiles**, beyond the two main samples used to set the dating model. Its results agreed with the existing dates within the stated uncertainty.
- The team applied it to a Shroud thread in **2022**. [Shroud application](https://doi.org/10.3390/heritage5020047)
- Earlier materials research already used WAXS to study damaged cellulose—for example, a **2006 study combining FTIR and WAXS**. That establishes a scientific foundation for measuring structural damage. [Study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0924203105001293)

**What does “accepted science” mean here?**

There is no single book where a paper becomes permanently approved. Publication means other scientists can examine and challenge it. Confidence grows when different teams successfully repeat the work.

These four approaches have published scientific foundations, and some encouraging checks. **The evidence I found for their specific linen-dating formulas is much smaller than the evidence supporting the instruments themselves.** That limits how confidently we can turn a reading into a calendar date; it does not erase their findings.
